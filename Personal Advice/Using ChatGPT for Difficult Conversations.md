## How ChatGPT helped me manage toxic conversations without emotional reactions

==I’ll be the first to admit that I use ChatGPT to respond to most of my emails====. It keeps me on the straight and narrow — polite, neutral, sprinkled with all the right (and overused) work email buzzwords.==

It is both time and energy-saving. Efficient, in other words, and all it requires is a prompt.

The wonders of technology.

You know what else is wonderful about it?

==I can respond to toxic people exactly the way I want to shut them down without making it seem rude or offensive.==

==Because ChatGPT can be a master at grey rocking — a communication strategy used to deal with toxic people by becoming as boring as a grey rock.==

It effectively puts the brake on the bullet train of their disruptive behavior.

Or better yet, as this article puts it so lyrically: “ *It’s a defensive tactic. It’s the emotional equivalent of playing dead so the would-be predator loses interest and moves on.*”

==How I wish ChatGPT were around during my difficult divorce negotiations. It would have handled all communications like a charm====.== ==It would also have analyzed emails from my ex, preparing me on how to deal with them swiftly and indifferently, instead of doubting myself and wondering if I was going crazy from all the manipulation going on.==

Of course, my lawyer helped too, but at $500 an hour, I was paying them a premium for something ChatGPT could easily have handled for free.

The thing is, we share kids, so back-and-forths between us about school and logistics are common. He uses every chance he gets to start an argument.

I get into defensive mode, and that is exactly what he is looking for.

==*It*== ==*was only later that I realized that any engagement, even a negative one, is a supply or emotional fuel that maintains their sense of high superiority and self-importance.*==

So instead of sending emotional replies, I started copying his texts into ChatGPT, asking it to compose a polite grey-rock response. ==And voila, it generates something factual, to-the-point, and non-challenging that stops further engagement==.

**It was either that or handing them my nervous system on a platter.**

Because more often than not, it felt like he was looking for a fight. In therapy, I learned about DARVO — Deny, Attack, and Reverse Victim and Offender**,** a pattern that explained this behavior.

Calling me a bad mom was the one thing that always got under my skin.

When left on holiday after making arrangements with him about the kids, he did a sudden 180. He intruded into what would have been a peaceful holiday, bombarding me with texts and emails, repeatedly calling me a bad mom for leaving my kids and prancing off into the sunset.

> The first one got to me, and I cried. It ruined my evening, and that was the intention all along.

I ignored the rest of the emails, and things were quiet after that.

Sometimes I feel the peace of mind I've gained has come at the cost of honesty.

==Maybe grey rocking works best when it’s temporary. What worries me is how easily it could become a habit — not a strategy, but as a default when I don’t feel like engaging at all.==

==**Perhaps the question is, at what point does protection become disconnection?**==
