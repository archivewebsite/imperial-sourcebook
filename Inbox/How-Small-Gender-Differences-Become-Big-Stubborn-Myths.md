## Or: why women and men aren’t as different as we like to believe

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*cNYSaD5GqRdZMl5RpL6WwQ.jpeg)

Image licensed from Adobe Stock.

[In a study](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0163-6383\(00\)00032-1) done right at the cusp of the millennium, a hundred and two newborns, most just a day and a half old, were presented with what was likely the first choice of their lives: would they rather look at the face of a female graduate student, or at a ball of roughly the same size mounted on a stick?

Among the 58 girls, the largest group — 27 (46%) — had no clear preference. Another 21 (36%) looked at the face for at least 10 seconds longer than the ball, while 10 (17%) favoured the ball. Among the boys, however, the largest group — 19 of 44 (43%) — preferred the ball, with 14 (31%) showing no preference and 11 (25%) favouring the face. Overall, both female and male babies spent roughly half of the one-minute observation period looking at the face.

Nonetheless, the study, conducted by clinical psychologist Simon Baron-Cohen alongside Jennifer Connellan (the graduate student in question) and other colleagues, concluded it showed ‘beyond a reasonable doubt’ that gender differences in sociability are ‘in part, biological in origin.’ It was then soon cited as proof that women are innately drawn to emotions and men to things, including by Leonard Sax in his popular book *Why Gender Matters.* It has, however, been criticised for its methodological flaws¹ at least as much (if not more) as it’s been praised.

Still, even *if* we assume the study was flawlessly executed, the effect size was hardly large enough to justify it snowballing into such a sweeping claim, especially the one about women being more socially oriented. Besides, many other studies show that girls and boys aren’t really consistently different from the very first hours of life.

For a few decades now, pop psychologists and cultural commentators have insisted that women and men are so unlike each other, it’s as if they belong to two entirely different species, or come from two entirely different planets, Venus and Mars². This idea also fits neatly with what’s been said about the sexes from antiquity through the early twentieth century. Men are, supposedly, the superior, large-brained beings with perfectly calibrated hormonal systems. ==Women, on the other hand, are plagued by all sorts of unfortunate biological realities, like their ‘wandering’ wombs occasionally rendering them emotionally unstable.==

Today, the persistent legacy of all of these more or less ancient beliefs doesn’t exactly make it easy for research pointing in the opposite direction to successfully cut through the fog of rigid gender binaries we’re all immersed in. But such research *does* exist.

One recent example is [a systematic review](https://doi.org/10.1111/sode.12790) published in *Social Development* that sifted through decades of studies from the 1960s onward, and across both medical and psychological databases. The researchers’ aim was to examine every scrap of research comparing boys’ and girls’ attention to stimuli in their very first month of life, deliberately not limiting their search to terms like ‘gender difference’ or ‘sex difference’ so that studies finding no differences were included as well.

==In total, they identified 40 experiments across 31 peer-reviewed studies, involving nearly 2,000 infants. The result of this meta-analysis was pretty straightforward: there was== ==*no significant*== ==difference between the genders.==

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*ENH6zoBo86UR5uvfrlbgXA.avif)

Distribution of time boys and girls spent looking at faces. Image from Lise Eliot’s article in The Conversation.

The researchers also investigated two other types of studies: those on newborns’ tendency to cry when they hear another baby cry, and those comparing babies’ responses to social versus inanimate objects, specifically using a newborn behaviour assessment scale. In the case of the former, there was, once again, no gender difference. In the latter case, girls paid slightly more attention to both social stimuli, such as a human face or voice, *and* inanimate stimuli, such as a ball or rattles.

As one of the study’s authors, neuroscientist Lise Eliot, writes in [*The Conversation*](https://theconversation.com/its-a-myth-that-baby-boys-are-less-social-than-girls-a-new-look-at-decades-of-research-shows-all-babies-are-born-to-connect-277453):

> So taken together, current research dispels a common myth that girls are innately ‘hardwired’ to be more social than boys in early life. The truth is that all babies are wired for social engagement at birth. Boys and girls are both primed to pay attention to human faces and voices, which, after all, belong to those who will keep them fed, safe and comforted.

One of the studies included in this review, based on an experiment similar to that conducted by Baron-Cohen and Connellan — though carried out with additional precautions³ to avoid the same methodological issues — actually did find gender differences. Just not *at first*. The initial experiment, done just a couple of days postpartum, showed no differences.

But in a follow-up experiment conducted three to four months later, gender differences began to show up.

There are many ways in which adult women and men tend to differ. Women, on average, [apologise more frequently](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610384150) than men. Men, on the other hand, are more likely to believe [they could safely land a passenger plane](https://thenoosphere.substack.com/p/why-half-of-men-think-they-could) in an emergency. There’s even a gender difference in who [uses more emojis](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.hcinlp-1.23) (women), who [eats more meat](https://thenoosphere.substack.com/p/how-traditional-masculinity-norms) (men), who’s more likely to give directions [using landmarks](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/sexual-personalities/201601/wheres-the-nearest-starbucks-sex-differences-in-wayfinding) (women), who [avoids asking for help](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.186.4.297) (men), and who’s more likely [to experience imposter syndrome](https://www.bbc.com/worklife/article/20200724-why-imposter-syndrome-hits-women-and-women-of-colour-harder) (women).

Cross-cultural personality studies show that, too. [One of the most frequently cited ones](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.81.2.322), published in 2001 inthe *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, involved over 23,000 women and men from 26 cultures. And it found that across these diverse cultures, women consistently rated themselves as warmer, friendlier, more anxious, and more sensitive than men did. Men, meanwhile, rated themselves as more assertive and open to new ideas. [Studies](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.88.3.547) that asked people not to describe themselves but rather a man or woman they knew very well found the same result.

However, *implicit* measures of personality — those that indirectly assess a person’s traits and attitudes, without relying on self-reporting — find much smaller gender differences. [A 2013 study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2013.08.008) involving over 14,000 participants found that gender differences in personality were *three* times smaller, or in some cases nonexistent, when measured implicitly than when measured with standard questionnaires.

One plausible explanation is that in explicit questionnaires, people are influenced by unconscious or conscious biases and a desire to fit in with what feels socially appropriate, leading to overstated gender differences. This effect can intensify further when participants are reminded, even if only subtly, of their gender before or during the task (that’s known as gender priming). Women, for example, perform worse on math tests when they are [reminded of their gender beforehand](https://doi.org/10.1006/jesp.1998.1373) or when they are [exposed to scientific claims](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1131100) about male biological superiority in maths.

Still, personality and cognitive abilities tests also often find something else: gender *similarities*. [A 2005 review](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066x.60.6.581) of 46 meta-analyses (yes, this was essentially a *meta* -meta-analysis) published in the *American Psychologist* found that across a wide range of variables — including mathematical problem solving, reading comprehension, attributions for success and failure, aggression, and leadership — gender differences were either small or negligible. As the study’s author, psychologist Janet Shibley Hyde, [notes:](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/comment?id=10.1371%2Fannotation%2F2aa4d091-db7a-4789-95ae-b47be9480338)

> (…) the overwhelming evidence, across multiple psychological domains, is that men and women are more similar than different; the distance between them is more like the distance between North Dakota and South Dakota \[than the distance between Mars and Venus\].

Hyde’s research also led her to formulate the gender similarities hypothesis, which holds that women and men, as well as boys and girls, are similar on most, but not all, variables and overall far more alike than different.

A couple of decades later, this hypothesis is also increasingly supported by neuroscience. [A 2021 meta-synthesis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2021.02.026) of three decades of human brain sex-difference findings, published in *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*,concluded that female and male brains are more similar than different. The brain, much like other organs such as the heart or kidneys, is largely ‘sexually monomorphic’ rather than ‘dimorphic,’ i.e., it comes in one general form and not two distinct types. As Eliot, who also contributed to this meta-synthesis, [points out:](https://www.rosalindfranklin.edu/news/massive-study-led-by-rosalind-franklin-university-neuroscientists-reveals-few-differences-between-men-and-women-s-brains/#:~)

> (…) the brain differences between large- and small-headed men are as great as the brain differences between the average man and woman. And importantly, none of these size-related differences can account for familiar behavioral differences between men and women, such as empathy or spatial skills.

Moreover, as both Eliot and many other researchers have noted, it’s usually the research that *does* show gender differences that’s [more likely to get published](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-23976-1), more likely to attract media and public attention, and, as a result, more likely to influence the stories we tell about women and men. Differences make for better headlines than similarities.

But if a gender difference is small or inconsistent, if it gets even smaller or disappears in more rigorous tests, or when you look at the full body of evidence and not just a couple of studies, how is it then unquestionably ‘innate’ or ‘hardwired’? And even when a difference shows up consistently, can we really claim it reflects fundamentally distinct cognitive or behavioural architectures, as long as our socio-cultural environments *also* continue to demand — and reward — those very differences?

Five years old. That’s the age at which girls and boys [already start to be concerned](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0002166) when they perceive they’re not a ‘normal’ or ‘proper’ member of their gender group.

What often follows is a rush to conform to those expectations, learned practically from the moment they came out of the womb⁴ and into the labyrinth of subtle or even invisible social norms dictating how they ought to act, speak, play, dress, and behave.

And these norms can be frightening to transgress, especially when we’re young. You fall in line, or else you’re the weirdo. For boys, these rules are arguably even harsher — femininity must be avoided at all costs, while masculinity must be *actively* proven and defended, hence the idea that manhood is ‘precarious.’ As a former weirdo girl myself, I can confirm my ‘boyishness’ on the other hand, wasn’t necessarily perceived as negative, at least not always.

But gender conformity doesn’t end with childhood. In adulthood, it continues to be reinforced by so many things around us, from [workplace segregation](https://thenoosphere.substack.com/p/gender-stereotypes-are-still-alive) and the expectations around domestic and care labour to countless other everyday cues, all reminding us which gender ‘team’ we belong to and urging us to join it — or suffer the consequences of not doing so. It also certainly doesn’t help that our brains are wired [to prefer stereotypical information.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.80.6.876) We more easily remember what fits the story (emotional girl, active boy) than what doesn’t (emotional boy, active girl).

Still, accepting that our sense of self, how we move through the world, the choices we make, the roles we adopt, etc., are shaped by our ideas about gender can feel unsettling. Some of us, after all, like to imagine we’re free-thinking individuals, entirely in charge of our own paths, or that we’re born just the way we are and remain like this until the day we die. For some men, acknowledging the influence of gender norms, and their fundamental similarity, and *humanity*, to women, can perhaps be even outright intolerable, because to recognise that women are like men is also to confront all the ways they have dehumanised and objectified women.⁵

Sure, humans come in all sorts of flavours, with some falling outside of the gender binary altogether. And it can be valuable to understand where differences do exist, and why, especially in the context of physical and mental health and safety.

Nevertheless, we’re one single species, with one evolutionary story, and one planet we call home, and we’re bound to share a lot of behaviours and instincts. Regardless of gender, humans are wired for connection and emotion. And regardless of gender, we possess a broad repertoire of cognitive and physical capacities to navigate this world.

It’s remarkable how easily small or ambiguous differences are inflated into big, essentialist narratives that confidently declare women and men to be worlds, or universes, apart, just because they align with what we already believe and because they (conveniently) justify existing inequalities. But there are many studies — including many *large* -scale reviews — that show this isn’t necessarily the case.

Scratch the surface of these often superficial gender differences, and you’ll find a mirror staring back. The myths persist only because we keep pretending the reflection isn’t there.
