## What They Understand That Most People Don’t

> What if the reason you can’t break your habits… is because you’re trying too hard?

We’ve been taught to fight cravings, resist urges, and control ourselves.

But the harder you push, the stronger they seem to come back.

There’s a Japanese way of approaching habits that flips this idea completely.

==Instead of fighting them, you observe them.==

And strangely… that’s what makes them disappear.

## The 20-Year Smoker’s Secret

My best friend had been smoking for like 20 years.

He tried everything to quit — nicotine patches, therapy, medication, even hypnosis. He spent years of his life and thousands of dollars trying to break the habit.

But nothing worked.

Then we traveled to Japan for work and met an old monk.

That conversation changed everything for him and for me also.

Within three months, he quit smoking completely — not through force, not through willpower, but through a different approach entirely.

One that felt almost too simple to be true.

It raises an uncomfortable question:

> ==What if everything we’ve been taught about breaking bad habits is wrong?==

## Why Fighting Habits Makes Them Stronger

Most of us treat our habits like enemies.

We fight them, even try to resist them, control them.

But what if that fight is the problem?

What if the act of resisting a habit is exactly what keeps it alive?

==In many Japanese philosophies, habits aren’t seen as enemies to defeat. They’re seen as patterns — patterns that once served a purpose.==

==Instead of using force, the idea is to work== ==*with*== ==your mind, not against it.==

And that changes everything.

## The Problem With Force

You’ve probably seen this pattern before.

We start strong like we take a new year resolutions to join a gym in January or delete social media apps and make big promises about becoming a “new person.”

And then… we fall back.

We’re not weak obviously but because we tried to force change too aggressively.

==Every habit — even destructive ones — began as a solution.==

- Smoking relieves stress
- Overeating soothes emotion
- Scrolling fills boredom

When you understand that, the habit stops looking like the enemy.

It becomes a signal.

## The Angry Monk

The monk we met in Japan told us that earlier in his life, he had a serious anger problem.

Like small frustrations would build throughout the day until he eventually exploded at his students.

He tried everything to control it — ==meditation, fasting, discipline.==

But nothing worked for him either actually made it worse.

The harder he fought his anger, the stronger it became then one day, an older monk gave him a simple instruction:

> “Stop trying to get rid of your anger. ==Just observe it.==”

So he did.

When anger arose, he didn’t suppress it, he noticed it:

- The heat in his chest
- The tension in his jaw
- The thoughts behind it

He simply watched and slowly, things changed.

The anger didn’t disappear overnight, but it lost its grip.

==By observing it without judgment, he created space between himself and the emotion.==

Over time, it came less often — and passed more quickly when it did.

## What a Craving Actually Feels Like

He told us one of the core ideas behind this approach is simple:

> Nothing lasts forever — not even cravings like nothing

- That urge for a cigarette
- That impulse to check your phone
- That sudden need for sugar.

They feel permanent in the moment *( like no I can’t control this)*

But they’re not.

Most people treat cravings like emergencies. They either give in immediately or try to distract themselves.

But there’s a third option:

Feel the craving, stay with it but don’t act on it.

Let it rise, let it pass.

Like a wave.

## The 10-Minute Rule

He told us to use this idea to quit smoking.

Instead of throwing away your cigarettes, just kept them nearby.

When the urge came, just would hold one, smell it fully experience the craving.

Then set a timer for 10 minutes.

And told yourself: *If I still want it after 10 minutes, I can smoke.*

During those 10 minutes, don’t distract yourself and don’t fight the urge just try observed it.

So my friend tried this and noticed something interesting:

The first few minutes were the worst it felt like he had to act, like he couldn’t sit still.

But then… by the time the timer ended, the urge usually passed not completely, but enough.

That’s when I realized that:

> You are not your cravings. You are the awareness that observes them.

## Small Changes Actually Work Better

Another thing they follow is keeping things small like really small.

Its the key idea is ***Kaizen*** — the practice of small, continuous improvement.

Instead of trying to change everything overnight, you change just a little.

==If you watch 4 hours of TV every night, don’t quit completely.==

Reduce it by 5 minutes.

That’s it…

It sounds almost too small to matter, but that’s the point.

Big changes trigger resistance and small changes slip through unnoticed.

Over time, those small shifts or changes rewire your habits naturally.

## Habits Fill Something

Habits don’t exist in isolation.

They fill a gap like:

- Most of the people smoke because we’re stressed
- Or scroll because we’re bored
- And overeat because we’re lonely.

If you remove the habit without addressing the need, something else will take its place.

The Japanese idea of *ikigai* — your reason for being — plays a role here.

==Instead of just removing a habit, you replace it with something meaningful.==

Not with any other distraction but with something real.

## Self-Compassion

One of the biggest mistakes people make is using shame as motivation.

We criticize ourselves when we fail like sometimes call ourselves weak and try to push harder.

But remember that shame doesn’t create change in fact it creates more conflict.

Part of you becomes the critic and another part becomes the rebel.

And you just get stuck in that loop

So the alternative is very simple but its not going to be easy:

==Juts be kind to yourself don’t be soft or careless just honest, without any kind of judgment.==

## Build New Paths

Breaking a habit isn’t about destroying something. It’s about building something new.

Think of habits like paths in a forest the more you walk one path, the clearer it becomes.

You can’t erase it overnight, but you can create a new path by walking it consistently, and over time, it becomes easier.

Meanwhile, the old path fades.

## Should You Try This?

Honestly… if you’re someone who keeps trying to “fix” your habits but ends up stuck in the same cycle.

Then definitely ***“A BIG YESS”***

This approach doesn’t ask you to be perfect and it doesn’t require extreme discipline or big life changes.

It just asks you to slow down for a moment.

To sit with the urge instead of immediately reacting to it.

You don’t need to follow every step perfectly even you don’t need to suddenly change everything.

Just start small.

Next time the urge shows up don’t rush to stop it, just notice it.

Give it a few minutes.

That alone can change more than you expect.
