## Books

Lately, and I don’t know if you know this feeling, but whenever life starts to feel too surface-level because I’m busy with everyday things, I automatically begin searching for depth again. And most of the time, I return to books. Sometimes novels, sometimes essays, and sometimes even textbooks that go deeply into a specific subject or question.

One of the books I recently started is *A World Appears* by Michael Pollan. He explores questions around consciousness, perception, and what it actually means for human beings to experience life internally, to feel, to observe, to be aware of the world at all. And somehow that’s exactly what I need right now.

I’m trying to read it slowly. Not because it’s difficult in a negative way, but because the book constantly makes me question my own understanding of consciousness. And I love that feeling, when a book doesn’t just give you information, but destabilizes the certainty of what you thought you already knew.

What I especially enjoy is how accessible the writing feels despite the complexity of the topic. And through the book, I keep discovering other scientists, philosophers, and researchers I’ve never heard of before. I always keep a loose sheet of paper inside the book, and whenever a new thinker or researcher is mentioned, I write their name down. Almost like building my own little literature archive. Later I look them up, read their essays, interviews, studies, or sometimes even their books. I think this has become one of my favorite parts of self-education, allowing one idea to naturally lead me into another.

Alongside that, I’ve also been reading *Notes to John* by Joan Didion.

And I honestly don’t fully know how to explain this, but from the moment I picked up this book, I felt an unusually deep emotional connection to it. Something I rarely experience with books. I love this book already, even though I haven’t properly read much of it yet.

I think part of it is my connection to Joan Didion herself. I remember reading *The Year of Magical Thinking*, and because it affected me so deeply, I finished it in a single day. Right after that, I immediately read *Blue Nights*. I haven’t read much more from her since then, but something about her honesty stayed with me. There’s a kind of emotional precision in the way she writes about grief, memory, fear, and human fragility that feels incredibly rare.

And I think that emotional empathy toward her created this strange attachment I now feel toward *Notes to John*. I have the feeling the book will be just as honest and raw. It revolves around her reflections from conversations with her psychiatrist and the way she navigates anxiety, grief, emotional memory, and loss.

## Topic: Handwriting & Personality

Lately, I’ve been wanting to explore handwriting psychology more deeply.

[As many of you know, I work a lot with handwriting and help people improve the way they write](https://raniagebagi.com/). But over time, I started noticing that handwriting says far more about a person than most people realize. Not only aesthetically, but psychologically, emotionally, even energetically.

And the more I observe people’s handwriting, the more patterns I begin to notice.

One thing I’ve seen again and again is how much a person’s confidence can shift once they begin to genuinely like their handwriting. It sounds small at first, almost superficial maybe, but it changes something internally. The way we write is such an intimate extension of ourselves. It’s movement, rhythm, pressure, emotion, identity, all translated onto paper without us fully noticing it.

For example, people who write very large often seem to allow themselves to take up more space in life as well. There’s a certain openness, expression, sometimes even boldness in the movement of the letters. Meanwhile, people with very small handwriting often appear more withdrawn internally, more observant, more likely to retreat into themselves.

And what fascinates me even more is how handwriting changes depending on emotional states.

Sometimes your writing becomes rushed when your nervous system is overwhelmed. Sometimes it becomes softer when you feel calm. Some people naturally lean their handwriting toward the right, which in graphology is often associated with movement toward the future, outward expression, openness toward life. While people whose writing leans more toward the left often appear more connected to the past, memory, reflection, or emotional hesitation.

These are all small observations, of course, but there are many of them. And while graphology isn’t always fully accepted academically as a formal science, there is still an entire field dedicated to studying these patterns and psychological connections.

And honestly, I find that incredibly interesting.

Because at its core, handwriting is not just writing. It’s movement shaped by consciousness. A physical trace of someone’s inner world.

And I think that’s why I feel so drawn toward studying it more deeply.
