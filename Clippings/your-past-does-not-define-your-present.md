> ==at some point, you’ll realise that in order to become someone you love, you have to stop being someone you know.==

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*hzbY2lQPRmw7hfM3.jpg)

This may be too obvious to say, but the past does not exist in the present. And yet, so many of us spend our lives dragging the same emotional baggage, year after year, until its weight becomes so familiar that letting go feels like shedding a fundamental piece of ourselves.

I’ve always wanted to leave my past behind, to be fully present, to live a life unchained from yesterday’s narrative. But often, I find myself mentally transporting back into the past despite my fierce intention to escape. I became a willing captive in my own prison of memories.

==There are many reasons why people hold on to the past. Some cannot release the unresolved emotions that cling to old events. Others feel such a fierce attachment to places, people, and memories that to let them go seems like erasing a part of themselves.== And then there are those who fear the vast uncertainty of what lies ahead — so they seek comfort in what is known, in what has already been written.

For me, it was always a mixture of all three.

==But I’ve come to learn that the past does not define the present. It shaped me, yes. It gave me soil to grow roots, language to name the world, and lessons that have carved me into who I am. But it is not a cage, nor is it a prophecy. It does not, should not, and cannot dictate what comes next — unless I choose to keep granting it that power==.

My past was messy, tangled with moments I wished I could undo — scenes where I longed to be braver, wiser, softer, or stronger. I convinced myself that if only I could examine those chapters more carefully, then maybe I could write something that I could be proud of.

So I kept wandering back into memory, combing through the ruins like an archaeologist of my own mistakes. I searched for what could be salvaged, what could be forgiven, what must be remembered, and what I should force myself to forget.

But reliving is not resolving. No matter how many times I replay the past, it cannot spare me from the inevitability of suffering. No matter how many lessons I’ve carved into my heart, I still let myself trust and be betrayed. No matter how many scars I’ve carried, I still love and get heartbroken. And I will do it a million times more.

==Because to shy away from pain is to reject the texture of life itself.==

Growth is just a constant iteration of destruction and restoration, a relentless cycle of shattering and rebuilding. There’s no final line, no last act of suffering before an eternal *happily ever after*.

As long as you’re breathing, you risk being in pain. As long as your heart still beats, it risks being broken. That risk is the cost of being alive, and the price of becoming whole.

My past is stitched into me; there’s no denying that. Every joy, every mistake, every wound and wonder has altered the shape of who I am. And I wore them like badges of survival, proof that I had endured, evidence that I had lived. Over time, they became such a big part of my sense of self that the thought of letting go felt like erasing my very identity.

But not everything we once carried needs to be carried forever. Just because something was once meaningful does not mean it must weigh us down for a lifetime.

We don’t have to cling to every detail, every conversation, every heartbreak to make sure that they were real. They were. They happened. And no amount of wishing — *if only it had been different, if only I could go back* — can bend the past into something else. ==What’s gone will never return, and living inside that ache only steals the breath of the present.==

==At some point, you’ll realise that in order to become someone you love, you have to stop being someone you know.== The formation of a new identity requires a sacrifice of an old one. It is scary, I know.

But think of what you’ve already endured, the storms you thought would break you that did not. You have survived far worse than this. Why don’t we do it again this time, but with the gentleness and grace of someone who finally knows they deserve to move forward?

My past is my home. I lived within its walls so long that its pain felt like safety.

When I read “The Courage to be Disliked”, there was this idea that startled me: we *choose* our suffering and our unhappiness. At first, that thought infuriated me. I never chose this. I never wanted to drown in an ocean of *what-ifs*. I tried so desperately to climb out of those waters, to free myself from their pull.

But then the book went on to explain:

> ==we chose to stay unhappy because it’s better to exist with justification than to live with responsibilities.==

To remain stuck in the past is, in a way, to remain justified. We can point to our wounds, our stories, our traumas, and say: *See, this is why I am the way I am.* To move on, however, is to carry no excuses, to live without a shield of justification, and that requires courage.

We cling to the past because no matter how painful it was, it was familiar. Our personality, our mindset, our views of life — all that we say we want to change — felt like home, even though it was an abusive one. And so we stayed, under a roof that hurt us, because it was still a roof, and we don’t know where else to go.

You didn’t choose what happened to you. But you can choose how to move on. The past may have built you, but it does not have to bind you.

I cannot forget the past; no human can. But I can learn to stop letting it drag me down.

I’ll see it as proof that I have grown, not a reminder that I was broken.  
I’ll hold it gently in my heart, not let it fester in my brain.  
I’ll acknowledge it as something that has happened, not the only thing that matters.

Life is still happening. Things change. Time moves forward. People move away.

> It’s time to move on, to let the past take its rightful place behind us, while we step into the flow of the present.
