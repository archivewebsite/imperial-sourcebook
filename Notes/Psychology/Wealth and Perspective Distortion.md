## Their success didn’t make them smarter, it made them blind

They’re not like us. They’re gods with broken brains and we’re the ones who anointed them.

Every time I hear someone wonder in horror how come people like Putin and his mega-wealthy supporters can just rain bombs upon innocent civilians, that’s my first thought: he’s not like us. His brain works by other laws and rules. Bombing innocent civilians is not a matter of morality. It’s a tool for getting what you want. The word “innocent” is uncalled for in this equation. Because their innocence is irrelevant.

Aren’t the animals we eat innocent? Of course they are. Do we still eat them? Most of us do. Is innocence a factor in this equation? Obviously not. It’s irrelevant.

None of us are anything more than an animal in a slaughterhouse for these people. The Putins, the Trumps, the Musks, the Zuckies and Thiels.

We’re a number, a tool. We can’t be more than that to them. Nor should we try.

The psychological and neurological disconnect between the ultra-wealthy and the rest of us is a gap impossible to bridge. ==And the fact that society reveres them as if they’re some sort of gods walking the earth, sources of wisdom to be followed by us mortals, is frankly ridiculous.==

Billionaires are the least qualified people to give life advice because their success has effectively broken their ability to perceive reality accurately. Or care about it genuinely.

==Society treats billionaires as all-knowing sages. We hang on their every word, follow their advice on productivity, ambition, and purpose, and assume that the kind of financial success they’ve achieved translates to deep insight into the human condition.== It doesn’t.

> Massive success is not massive insight. It’s massive success. That’s it. And the two aren’t just different things — they’re often in direct conflict.

The evidence that wealth distorts perception isn’t a theory, or my personal hot take, for the sake of doom porn. It’s documented, repeatable science.

==Social psychologist Paul Piff has found that as a person’s wealth increases, their feelings of compassion and empathy go down, while their feelings of entitlement and self-interest go up. (====TED====)==

His team ran study after study proving it. In one now-famous experiment, researchers catalogued passing cars by price at a Berkeley intersection, then watched driver behavior. ==Drivers of expensive cars were consistently more likely to ignore pedestrians at crosswalks.==

In lab settings, wealthier participants were more likely to cheat on tests to win a prize they didn’t need, hoard resources, and bend rules the moment it suited them.

They were also more likely to interrupt people mid-sentence, ignore social norms around communication, and be outright rude.

> Oh, and the wealthy shoplifted more than the poor. People who could buy the store. Stealing lip balm. The entitlement is embarrassing.

I’ve had wealthy tenants who refused to leave, threatened me, and left my apartment a pigsty. In the meantime I noticed they also stole my champagne glasses. I left there a set of six champagne glasses. There was nothing special about them — they were random, cheap glasses you can buy anywhere. And they took them. These people who can afford a daily case of that fancy champagne I can never pronounce… they wanted my stupid ugly crummy glasses.

Embarrassing.

And there’s one more layer to this. If we zoom out from behavior to biology, it stops being about character and starts being about hardware.

Neuroscientist Sukhvinder Obhi found that wealth suppresses the mirror neuron system — the part of the brain that fires when you watch someone else suffer, the part that makes you feel it. (Rolling Stone)

I don’t mean theoretically. Biologically. Physically. The neurons that are supposed to bridge you to another person’s experience just quiet down. High status and high power just reduce that activity in the premotor cortex.

> So when a billionaire watches the news and seems unmoved — he’s not hiding it. There’s just neurologically nothing to hide.

Then there’s the question of what reality even looks like from up there.

Two psychologists, Kahneman and Tversky, spent decades studying how human judgment goes wrong, and one of their findings is very relevant here.

People don’t assess how likely something is by thinking carefully. That’s not how people work. They assess it by how quickly an example pops into their head. That’s it. That’s the whole mechanism. Your brain isn’t running a probability calculation; it’s asking — does this feel familiar?

And whatever feels familiar gets treated as normal. Whatever doesn’t, gets treated as rare, or unlikely, or the person’s own fault.

A rich person’s brain is running the same software. But the examples it has on file are entirely different from yours. Basically people who made it, other winners, other people the system smiled at. Failure doesn’t come to mind easily when it’s never been their failure.

So it starts to feel like a personal defect in whoever experiences it — a character flaw, laziness, lack of hustle, etc.— rather than what it usually is: math, circumstance, and a system that was never designed with us in mind.

This loss of perspective isn’t even a decision, it’s an automatic psychological consequence of accumulating power.

Which means rich people are not choosing to be obtuse. They’ve just been at altitude long enough that they’ve forgotten what ground level looks like.

## Why Money Turns People Into Monsters

### Maybe it’s more than human nature

medium.com

In study after study, high-power participants were worse at reading emotions, worse at imagining other people’s vantage points, and consistently more convinced that their own perspective was the accurate one.

So when a billionaire tells you that failure is a personal choice, they might genuinely believe it. That’s the whole problem.

Because here’s what they believe about success: that they deserve it. And if you don’t have it, it’s because you don’t deserve it. This has been studied extensively by Dacher Keltner, a renowned expert in human emotion. What did he find?

> The wealthy consistently explain their success in terms of their own superior traits: drive, talent, making the right decisions. The rest of us, with somewhat more contact with actual reality, recognize the role of timing, luck, structural barriers, and the people who helped along the way.

Both groups are rationalizing their position. But only one group has the platforms, the publicists, and the net worth to make their rationalization sound like wisdom.

If a billionaire has only ever won, they start to believe that failure is a choice: not a statistical possibility or a structural outcome. To him, it’s a personal deficiency in people who didn’t want it enough.

That’s the cruelest part of the billionaire advice industrial complex. It doesn’t just fail the people who follow it — it actively obscures the mechanisms that keep people stuck.

> Every time someone tells you to wake up at 5AM, journal your goals, or just believe harder, that’s a billionaire’s worldview laundered into productivity content. It keeps you looking inward for solutions to problems that are structural. It keeps you optimizing yourself while the systems that limit you go unquestioned, unchanged, and extremely profitable for someone else.

And yet we keep asking them. We keep scrolling their feeds, buying their books, watching their interviews, asking them how to fix the world they broke, and treating their presence at Davos like a sacred gathering of the wisest people on earth.

About that Davos thing — it gets almost comedic if it weren’t so ridiculous.

The World Economic Forum meets every January in Davos, Switzerland. Entry costs around $29,000 per person. The attendees are heads of state, central bank governors, and the CEOs of the world’s largest corporations. ==They spend five days in panel discussions about poverty, inequality, climate change, the state of democracy, and women’s empowerment — topics that, notably, their own business decisions directly contribute to.== It’s a gathering not of the 1%, but of the 0.000001%.

==And then, every year,== ==escort agencies across Europe report a record surge in demand====, with sex workers traveling from across the continent to service the attendees, while the clients protect themselves with NDAs.==

In 2025 alone, around 300 women and trans women were booked in Davos and surrounding areas — up from 170 the year before.

==Sex parties, orgy nights, and prostitutes.== ==That’s Davos. That’s the sacred gathering.==

The gap between the stated agenda and the reality is flabbergasting.

And yet we keep asking them. We keep looking to them for answers to our deepest life questions. That’s what we need to stop first: holding them in high regard. Imagining they know anything.

==So let’s stop it. Out of self-respect. Or resentment — it’s a reasonable response in this situation.==

Let’s stop handing authority over our life to people who live in a neurological and economic reality so detached from ours that their advice is, at best, irrelevant, and at worst, deliberately designed to keep us from asking the right questions.

The science is clear: power erodes empathy, wealth narrows perception, and success at that scale has a measurable cost to how accurately someone reads the world.

==Admire the scale of what they’ve built, if you must.== I do. And I don’t even blame them for the depravity. I blame us for pretending it isn’t happening. But let’s stop treating them like they see something you don’t. They see less. They just have more power and a bigger mouth.

> ==And until we decide to actually do something about that — tax them. Hard. Davos-hard. It’s for their own good. And for ours.==
