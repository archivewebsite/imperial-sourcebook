Here’s the research-backed version of the advice:

## Become a useful generalist, not a wandering trivia goblin

The strongest version of “become a generalist” is not “learn a little about everything forever.” That is how people accumulate browser tabs instead of ability. The better rule is:

**Go wide to discover patterns, then go deep where leverage, risk, or execution demands it.**

Research supports this balance. James March’s classic work on organizational learning argues that adaptive systems need both **exploration** and **exploitation**: exploration gives you search, experimentation, play, discovery, and innovation; exploitation gives you refinement, execution, efficiency, and implementation. Too much exploration produces undeveloped ideas; too much exploitation traps you in stale competence. Human civilization, naturally, keeps trying both mistakes in spreadsheet form. ([Internet Archive](https://archive.org/download/wikipedia-scholarly-sources-corpus/10.1287.zip/10.1287%252Forsc.2.1.71.pdf "Exploration and Exploitation in Organizational Learning"))

## Why breadth matters

Breadth helps because innovation often comes from recombination. A major _Science_ study of 17.9 million research papers found that the highest-impact work was usually grounded in conventional knowledge but included unusual combinations; papers with that blend were twice as likely to become highly cited. The lesson is not “be random.” It is “build enough conventional competence to be understood, then import something unexpected.”

Interdisciplinary work also tends to be undervalued too early. A PLOS ONE study found that interdisciplinary variety and distance were positively associated with long-term citation impact, while short-term metrics could underestimate that impact. This is a useful warning: cross-domain work often looks messy before it looks obvious. ([PLOS](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0127298 "Interdisciplinarity and Impact: Distinct Effects of Variety, Balance, and Disparity | PLOS One"))

Breadth also strengthens analogy. Cognitive research on analogical reasoning shows that people learn and solve problems by mapping relational structures from one context to another. That is basically the respectable academic version of “patterns repeat across fields,” minus the hoodie-and-espresso mysticism. ([Northwestern Psychology Groups](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/gentner%26Smith_2013.3b.pdf "Analogical Learning and Reasoning - Oxford Handbooks"))

## Why depth still matters

The evidence does **not** say “avoid specialization.” It says specialization should be selected, not blindly inherited. A 2025 _Nature Human Behaviour_ briefing describes human capital as nested: specialized skills often depend on broader foundational skills and knowledge. So the generalist advantage comes from building a strong base, then adding deep spikes where they matter. ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-02094-1 "Skill dependencies underlie career paths and have societal implications | Nature Human Behaviour"))

McKinsey makes the same practical point with the “T-shaped” skill profile: broad general capabilities plus one or more deep areas of specific expertise. Annoyingly corporate, but not wrong. ([McKinsey & Company](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/operations/our-insights/operations-blog/ops-40-the-human-factor-a-class-size-of-1 "T-shaped skills profiles and the human factor"))

## Replace “no plan” with “adaptive direction”

“Follow the fun” is useful, but incomplete. Curiosity genuinely helps learning: a Psychological Science study found that higher curiosity was linked to reward-system activation and better recall of surprising answers one to two weeks later. ([Neuroeconomics](https://neuroecon.berkeley.edu/papers/papers_files/Kang_PsychSci_2009.html "The Wick in the Candle of Learning: Epistemic Curiosity Activates Reward Circuitry and Enhances Memory"))

But curiosity without feedback becomes intellectual grazing. Self-determination theory research shows that autonomy, competence, and relatedness support stronger motivation and learning outcomes, which means the best learning path is self-directed **and** structured enough to build competence. ([Stial](https://stial.ie/resources/Ryan%20and%20Deci%202020%20self%20determination%20theory.pdf "Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation from a self-determination theory perspective_ Definitions, theory, practices, and future directions"))

So the upgraded rule is:

**Follow the fun, but make the fun produce artifacts.**

A project. A model. A prototype. A memo. A tool. A dataset. A diagram. A shipped feature. Something outside your skull, because the skull is a terrible project management system.

## Practical advice

1. **Pick a problem domain, not a curriculum.**  
    Do not start with “I will learn math, physics, economics, biology, software, and systems.” That is how ambition gets embalmed. Start with a recurring problem you care about, such as “build better tools for decision-making,” “understand markets,” “make biological systems computable,” or “design AI products people can actually use.”
    
2. **Build a broad base of transferable tools.**  
    Prioritize skills that travel: statistics, basic programming, writing, systems thinking, economics, psychology, design, and scientific reasoning. These let you enter new fields faster because they give you reusable mental handles.
    
3. **Use projects as the learning engine.**  
    Project-based and problem-based learning have decent empirical support. A meta-analysis of 50 studies in higher education found problem-based learning had a significant positive effect on critical thinking, and a separate meta-analysis of 66 project-based learning studies found improvements in learning outcomes, academic achievement, attitudes, and thinking skills. So yes, solving real problems works better than passively hoarding courses, a shocking development for the lecture-industrial complex. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1871187122000724 "Effects of problem-based learning instructional intervention on critical thinking in higher education: A meta-analysis - ScienceDirect")) ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1202728/full "Frontiers | A study of the impact of project-based learning on student learning effects: a meta-analysis study"))
    
4. **Go deep only when depth removes a bottleneck.**  
    Depth is worth stacking when a topic blocks execution, gives compounding returns, reduces costly mistakes, or creates a durable advantage. Otherwise, learn enough to reason, collaborate, and move.
    
5. **Create a pattern library.**  
    Every time you learn something, ask: “Where else does this structure appear?” Feedback loops appear in biology, economics, software, and climate. Optimization appears in machine learning, logistics, investing, and evolution. Incentives appear in organizations, politics, markets, and family group chats, tragically.
    
6. **Alternate exploration and execution.**  
    Spend some periods sampling new domains, then force a delivery cycle. For example: four weeks exploring, eight weeks building. Exploration keeps you fresh; execution keeps you honest.
    
7. **Make learning durable.**  
    Do not just read and highlight. A major review of learning techniques found that practice testing and distributed practice have high utility across learners and tasks, while rereading and highlighting are much weaker. In other words: quiz yourself, revisit ideas over time, and stop pretending neon underlines are memory. ([Gwern](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2013-dunlosky.pdf "Improving Students’ Learning With Effective Learning Techniques: Promising Directions From Cognitive and Educational Psychology"))
    

## Revised principle

**Do not become a generalist by avoiding depth. Become a generalist by collecting useful lenses, then applying them deeply enough to build things specialists alone cannot see.**

Breadth gives you range.  
Depth gives you force.  
Artifacts prove you are not just aesthetically interested in being clever.
