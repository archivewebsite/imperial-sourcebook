Can every integer be expressed as the sum of four integer cubes?

Not known. It is **conjectured** that every integer can be written as

$$
n = a^3 + b^3 + c^3 + d^3
$$

with integers $a, b, c, d$, including negative integers, but this has not been proved. Number theory, naturally, looked at a simple-looking question and turned it into a haunted attic.

What is known: every integer **not congruent to $4$ or $5 \pmod 9$** is a sum of four signed cubes. Henri Cohen’s page describes Demjanenko’s result as proving that any integer “not 4 or 5 modulo 9” is a sum of positive or negative cubes. ([Math Bordeaux][1])

The unresolved cases are therefore

$$
n \equiv 4 \text{ or } 5 \pmod 9,
$$

also written $n \equiv \pm 4 \pmod 9$. This is not a modular impossibility for four cubes, since four residues $1 + 1 + 1 + 1 \equiv 4 \pmod 9$ are possible. It is just where the current methods fail, because apparently civilization needed another reminder that “no obvious obstruction” is not the same thing as “proof.” A Cambridge article describes it as an old conjecture that every integer can be decomposed into four positive or negative perfect cubes. ([Cambridge Core][2])
