## the Vaguer They Are, the Less They’re Worth

Think back to that first weekend you spun up an Obsidian vault.

You probably spent hours fussing with folder hierarchies, trying on community themes, installing the then–top-ranked Dataview, and later Excalidraw, Kanban, Calendar, and Tasks. At some point you stared at the pristine global Graph View, daydreaming about the gorgeous “second brain” or knowledge garden it would surely become.

Then, after a few half-baked notes, came the ceremonial moment: you typed your first pair of double brackets \[\[ \]\].

What should you reference or create? “202403 Podcast Jottings”? “Atomic Habits, Chapter 2 Notes”? “A Few Ideas on Creativity”?

It doesn’t take long before you hit Ctrl+O (or Cmd+O), type “creativity,” and watch dozens of fuzzy, unrelated results tumble out — only to wonder, what on earth is all this?

And that graph? It’s not a “neural network.” Frankly, that metaphor has misled a lot of people. I’m convinced the brain doesn’t work like that — and mapping out a cool web of nodes isn’t the same as mastering how knowledge connects.

After a few years of this, I’m confident the root of the chaos starts at the very beginning: **how we name our notes.**

## Why, in Obsidian, the Title Is Everything

Traditional note apps leaned on tree-style folders — a carryover from older file systems. In Obsidian, the core mechanic is bidirectional links. That doesn’t mean folders and tags don’t matter; it means links are first-class citizens, and everything else supports how notes connect.

But whether you rely on links, folders, or tags, your ability to connect notes is still tightly coupled to titles. Two years from now, when you’re hunting for a point to back a new article, the thing that will most likely reunite you with an old note is a title that happens to match the very term your brain naturally surfaces.

We won’t re-read every note in the future. What actually bubbles up in our minds are short titles and keywords — call them tags if you like.

That’s our biological brain doing information compression: building a mental “short index.” That short index is closer to the knowledge we truly hold than any flashy “second brain” visualization.

## Common Title Traps

If you don’t think about titles up front, you’re guaranteed to feel the pain later. Here’s what I learned the hard way.

## Trap 1: Naming by Source

It’s a comforting illusion. We feel organized because we’re tracking where something came from. But “source” is only one dimension at the moment of collection. And now, in 2026, Obsidian plugins and AI are mature enough to capture source metadata automatically the second you clip it. Repeating it in the title adds little value — unless the source itself carries special significance.

## Trap 2: Vague Titles

Things like \[\[Thoughts on Memory\]\] or \[\[Reflections on Habits\]\] aren’t titles; they’re placeholders.

With 50 notes, this might fly. With 500, it falls apart. You’ll only match generic words — “memory,” “habits,” “thoughts” — with no signal. At citation time, you won’t know if you’ve hit the mark without re-reading the note, which kills efficiency. A good title helps you decide, instantly, what to read and what to skip.

A better title would be: \[\[Self-testing beats rereading for long-term retention\]\].

Also, lean into Obsidian’s strengths: write more atomic notes. Don’t throw a heap of content into one page and slap on a catch-all title. I used to do this a lot; it backfires.

## Trap 3: Using Questions Instead of Answers

\[\[What is antifragility?\]\] or \[\[How does compounding really work?\]\]

Questions feel engaging — but they don’t build strong connections.

A question isn’t knowledge; it’s a doorway into your to-research queue. As a placeholder for a note you intend to investigate, a question can be fine. But as an archived note title, it’s weak.

If you’ve thought it through, write the conclusion: \[\[Antifragile systems benefit from volatility, not merely withstand it\]\].

That’s a full claim. It can be debated, extended, and linked from other notes. If you don’t know the answer yet, don’t archive it — keep it in your inbox or a Todo queue.

By my logic above, if you can state that conclusion, it’s because you’ve already done the work of compression; it’s now in your brain’s short index.

## Trap 4: Abusing Zettelkasten IDs

Beginners enamored with Luhmann’s Zettelkasten often prefix notes with timestamps, e.g., \[\[202403281423–Deliberate Practice\]\]. Obsidian even has a core plugin for this.

Luhmann used numeric IDs because he had physical cards in a wooden box — he needed a way to anchor notes in physical space. We, however, have millisecond search.

In digital tools, keep timestamps in YAML metadata if you like, but don’t let them hog the visual center of the title — unless time itself is essential (say, for current events). If you must use a prefix to avoid collisions, make sure what follows is a complete idea, not a blank or withered noun.

## Trap 5: Overreliance on Web Clippers

Old news, but worth repeating: digital hoarding isn’t knowledge. No matter how much you stash.

A typical pattern: you sync highlights from a web clipper, Readwise, or Omnivore straight into Obsidian. The system auto-titles the page with the article’s name and dumps dozens of highlights below. The page looks polished; you close it, satisfied.

The note exists, but the thinking hasn’t happened. You simply relocated information from the internet to your hard drive. For retrieval, you might be better off not saving it at all — just Google it later, or ask an AI.

My fix: don’t let clippings enter the knowledge vault. I keep a separate vault for saves — a “read-and-burn” drawer. I know everything there is ephemeral; if it sits too long, I purge it. If something proves valuable, I distill it: I convert it into a note with a conclusion-style title and only the essential points — not a full paste. Distillation forces me to produce my own title, and with it, my own thinking.

## “But Isn’t This Too Hard?”

Once you try “thesis-style” naming, you’ll discover a harsh truth: more than half your notes may not contain an extractable claim.

Worse, every time a spark hits, you’re supposed to open Obsidian, wait for a pile of plugins to load, and wring out the perfect title? That kind of friction breeds perfectionist paralysis — and you end up capturing nothing.

If you’re stuck here, my advice is blunt: don’t do every step in Obsidian.

Just as I isolate clippings in a separate place, I also write elsewhere. My Obsidian vault is an archive. Only things with lasting value — things I’ve actually thought about — go in. By then, a good title comes easily.

Physically separate “collection” and “thinking.”

## In Closing

This may sound like a post about note titles, but the real topic is thinking.

- Collection exists to feed thinking — not to hoard. During capture, you often can’t title well.
- Classification exists to decide the order, levels, and structure of thinking. Don’t turn a single classification facet into a title.
- Distillation exists to produce results. That’s when a title should appear.
- Publishing (or sharing) re-views and reorganizes outcomes. That’s when even better titles emerge.

No matter how good AI summaries, smart search, or future tech becomes, notes are here to strengthen our first brain. Any tool that tempts us to outsource thinking to a so‑called “second brain” is risky.

The best way to test and deepen your thinking is to start with how you name your notes. Open your current Obsidian vault and go through your notes, one by one. Can you give each of them a good title?
