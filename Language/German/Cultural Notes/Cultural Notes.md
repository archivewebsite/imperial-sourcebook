# Cultural Notes

These notes focus on practical communication choices in German.

| Topic | Practical Note | Example | Indonesian Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `Guten Tag` as safe greeting | Use a full greeting in polite or service contexts. | `Guten Tag, ich hätte gern...` | Lebih aman daripada langsung meminta. |
| `du` vs `Sie` | Use `Sie` with adults you do not know in formal settings. | `Können Sie mir helfen?` | `du` shows familiarity. |
| Capital `Sie` | Formal `you` is capitalized in writing. | `Haben Sie Zeit?` | This helps distinguish from `sie`. |
| First names | First names often go with `du`; last names and titles often go with `Sie`. | `Herr Müller`, `Frau Schneider` | Follow the context. |
| Directness | German can sound direct compared with English, but politeness still matters. | `Ich brauche Hilfe.` | Direct does not always mean rude. |
| `bitte` flexibility | `bitte` can mean please, here you go, pardon, or you're welcome. | `Bitte schön.` | Meaning depends on situation. |
| Punctuality language | Time commitments are usually stated clearly. | `um 10 Uhr`, `gegen 10 Uhr` | `gegen` is approximate; `um` is exact. |
| Classroom questions | Asking for repetition is normal and useful. | `Können Sie das bitte wiederholen?` | Good learner phrase. |
| Service interactions | Short, polite phrases are common. | `Ich hätte gern einen Kaffee.` | `Ich will` can sound too blunt. |
| Privacy | Personal questions may need softening. | `Darf ich fragen...?` | Ask permission for sensitive topics. |
| Regional variation | German-speaking countries and regions vary in vocabulary and habits. | `Tschüss`, `Servus`, `Grüezi` | Learn local forms when needed. |
| False friends and humor | Similar-looking words can create misunderstandings. | `Gift` means poison, not present. | Treat false friends as language-culture traps. |
