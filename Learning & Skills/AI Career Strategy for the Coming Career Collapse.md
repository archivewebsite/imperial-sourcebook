# AI Career Strategy for the Coming Career Collapse

## “Time-for-security” vs. “value-for-relevance.”

There’s a new dread in town. It’s “career anxiety.” And it’s everywhere people have a 9–5 job. It was never meant to be a source of existential anxiety. For most of the 20th century, it was the solution. You go to work, get the job done, and the company looked after you. You get your pensions. And promotion if you stick around long enough. The deal was basically: ==give us your time, and we’ll give you security.== Millions of people built their entire lives around that agreement. Don’t get me wrong. It still works for most people. But right now, people are more anxious about that arrangement. The trouble is, that’s how we were taught to think about work. Go to school. Get your certificates. Pick a stable career. Find a reputable employer. Stay loyal. And keep climbing. Of course, it was solid advice in 1975. In 2026, it’s making people doubt their very way of making a living.

What got us here may not get us into the future.

Tariff wars are rattling supply chains and pushing companies into survival mode. Tech companies that hired aggressively ten years ago have spent the last two years cutting. And tech intelligence is making it even more easier to keep cutting. Goldman Sachs predicts that north of 300 million full-time jobs are at risk. I’m thinking the worst-case scenario for many career paths. Most career anxiety is also creating identity crisis. And disorienting experiences. And different from ordinary stress. “I’m not sure the thing I’m doing still matters,” is what’s going through people’s minds. It’s insane how fast things are changing. A policy decision made in Washington can restructure global supply chains within months.

Companies that were profitable in January become cost-cutting machines by the middle of the year. The people who get caught in those cuts didn’t do anything wrong. They just happened to have a skill set that became inconvenient at exactly the wrong time. Tech intelligence is also taking tasks off your plate, until one day, the tasks are all gone.

And so is the justification for your role.

You’ve probably heard the advice: build transferable skills. Stay adaptable. Be a lifelong learner. It’s not bad advice. But it’s incomplete. Problem-solving or collaboration skills are also table stakes. Everyone is now trying to get them or claims to. They don’t protect you. They will get you in the room, though. ==What protects you is “specific knowledge.”== Something that’s hard to acquire or take off your plate. And valuable enough that someone will keep paying for it even when budgets are tight. There’s a difference between “marketing experience” and a better knowledge of “customer psychology.” And conversion details well enough to diagnose why a campaign failed. Even the ability to apply tech tools to real life problems in a specific industry is not the same as working well with them.

==“Specific knowledge is found by following your genuine intellectual curiosity, better than whatever is hot right now. If the society can train you, it can train someone else, and replace you.” — Naval Ravikant==

## The new career logic

A better framework for thinking about your life in the next ten years is this: forget the ladder. Focus on value creation as three overlapping circles.

- Circle one: what the world needs now and may still need tomorrow. That means paying attention to where money and effort are moving. Where genuine problems exist that need solving.
- Circle two: what can become so good at that they can’t touch. Not what you’re interested in. What you can master that requires multiple tools to figure out.
- Circle three: what you can communicate without struggle. Can youexplain your expertise clearly It’s a skill if you know how to package it for the right audience. People who can write, speak, and teach what they know have a massive advantage (leverage) over equally skilled people who can’t.

The sweet spot is where those three circles overlap.

That’s where you want to be focusing.

For example, a specialised healthcare compliance analyst is more than a technical expert. She understands GDPR, industry governance, and clinical audits. She can bridge the gap between IT and legal teams. This rare skillset cannot be taught. It’s built over years of paying attention to one problem space. A writer who got obsessed with the psychology of behaviour change and learned to apply it across health, finance, and education is not just a copywriter anymore. People will work with her for the “specific knowledge” she brings to the table. She sees things they don’t. These are not extraordinary people. They just made a decision to go deep on something specific that no one can easily take away.

They protected themselves.

Some jobs are going to disappear regardless of how skilled you are. Many industries are going to get disrupted faster than anyone predicted. And specific knowledge may not completely protect you. But if you build something that’s hard to replicate, whether that’s expertise, reputation, relationships, or some combination of all three, you are a step ahead of disruption. The 9–5 worked because it offloaded the uncertainty. You gave up agency in exchange for predictability. That rule made sense when companies could deliver on predictability. They can’t anymore. The uncertainty is back. But you can invest in “specific leverage” to get rid of some of the work anxiety people feel right now.

“If you are deliberately trying to create a future that feels safe, you will willfully ignore the future that is likely,” says Seth Godin.

The old model of work is failing people.

The uncertainty and anxiety are getting worse.

You need to hone in on something specific. Now the harder question is: on *what*, exactly “Build specific knowledge” is easy to say and genuinely difficult to execute. The frameworks out there are confusing. Start with the three-circle logic I explained earlier. It’s more important now than ever. I will help you think clearly about *why* certain capabilities will hold their value over the next decade.

## “Value-for-relevance” is replacing “time-for-security”

“The best jobs are neither decreed nor degreed. They are creative expressions of continuous learners in free markets.” — Naval

Right now, tech intelligence is extraordinarily good at pattern completion. It excels at tasks that can be defined, repeated, and evaluated against specific standards. If your job is mostly pattern completion. Taking a known input and producing a known output, that job will be under real pressure. It may not necessarily go tomorrow. But it could get absorbed into tools that cost a fraction of a salary. Nobody wants that. The good news is that tech intelligence now struggles with ambiguity. It can’t reliably exercise judgment where there are no established patterns. It doesn’t build trust. It can’t walk into a room and read what’s actually going on. It can’t figure out a human problem with no clear or tangible output.

That’s where you come in.

The ‘human’ elements of work, building relationships, understanding emotions, and making tough choices, are now more important than ever. Those are human capabilities. And they become *more* valuable as the pattern-completion work is taken off the table. The skills that compound in your favour. The capabilities that transfer across domains and appreciate over time, the longer you build them, the more valuable they become.

**Judgment in complex work processes.** Every system, a company, a hospital, a government department, a supply chain, is a system. Systems break in non-obvious ways. They have feedback loops, hidden dependencies. And cultural dynamics people don’t see on paper. The ability to look at a complex system and identify what’s wrong or could go wrong is one of the most valuable and least teachable skills there is. It takes years of exposure. Pattern recognition is built through experience. The willingness to ask uncomfortable questions. You can’t give that to AI. You can augment it with better data tools.

But the good judgment is a human task.

If you’ve spent years inside a specific industry, healthcare, logistics, financial services, or education, you probably have more of this than you realise. The question is whether you’re treating it as expertise. Or better still, can you present it as leverage

**A bridge between people who see the world in different ways.** Few people can do this anymore: step into the space between two experts and translate their worlds. Can you make two different kinds of minds understand each other The technical and non-technical. Creative and commercial. Legal and management. Most companies are full of brilliant people who can’t communicate across these divides. Meetings go in circles. Projects stall. Decisions get made on bad information.

People can’t properly explain knowledge to the person who needs it most in the best way possible. If you can make a CFO understand why a technical decision has financial benefits, or make an engineering team understand why a user experience detail matters, you are worth a great deal.

Become a bridge builder of expert knowledge.

This is a learnable skill. But requires curiosity about domains outside your own. And the discipline to learn them well enough to reason in them. You don’t have to master them. But you can learn to think inside the domain. E.O. Wilson was right, “We are drowning in information, while starving for wisdom. The world henceforth will be run by synthesizers, people able to put together the right information at the right time, think critically about it, and make important choices wisely.”

**Taste and discernment.** As AI makes it easy to produce *competent* work: competent writing, design and code. The ability to tell the difference between competent and good becomes more valuable. Someone has to be able to look at a dozen AI outputs and know which one is right. Someone has to have the editorial judgment to know when something is almost there and what it needs. Taste is a skill. It’s built through sustained exposure to excellent work, active critical engagement with what you consume. And the development of your own point of view. Writers who read great work and develop taste for what’s worth anyone’s time. Designers who study good design and can discern what’s better. Product managers who understand what makes products work develop taste. It’s hard to fake taste or automate. You either have it or you don’t.

**Thinking across boundaries.** The next decade’s hardest problems won’t be inside a single discipline. ==The ability to see patterns that specialists, buried in their own fields, tend to miss will be rare. The best technology thinking requires engineering, economics, policy, and behavioural science. People who can think rigorously across two or more of these domains have something most people don’t. The specialist knows everything about their field and nothing useful about adjacent ones.== The generalist knows a little about everything and can’t go long on anything. What the next decade rewards is something different. Good knowledge in at least one area, combined with the intellectual curiosity to understand and reason in adjacent ones. Become the root system, go further in some places. And wider enough to draw from unexpected sources.

**Also, the ability to build trust at scale.** This last one is the hardest to acquire. But pays off over the long run. Trust is the foundation of every transaction, relationship, and many systems. But right now it’s very scarce. People have lost trust in the institutions meant to keep us safe and stable. Institutions are less trusted than they were. People are more sceptical of facts or claims. And more uncertain who to believe. With that in mind, the ability to build genuine trust. Through consistency, demonstrated competence, and authentic presence is a valuable asset.

Whether you’re a consultant, an employee, an entrepreneur, or a creator, the people who go the long mile are the ones people trust. Trust compounds. A reputation built over years, through hundreds of small honest actions, is one of the best assets you can have. It’s also one of the hardest things for anyone or any algorithm to replicate.

Most people understand our 21st-century crisis, but they are stuck consuming knowledge. They are aware of the urgency to take control. But they can’t help but keep reading. And taking courses. But then six months later, they realise they haven’t converted anything to practical wisdom for life or ability. They’ve become more anxious because they understand the problem better.

The decision to build something practical. Or get serious with all that knowledge, is how you take control. Build capacity in anything you want to make your “specific leverage.” A body of work. A set of skills. Our 21st-century crisis has only just begun. It’s not complete. Most companies are still figuring out how to use tech intelligence effectively. Most industries are still in the early stages of adaptation. The people who develop strong, specific capabilities over the next three to five years will be entering a market where the demand for exactly those capabilities is at its highest. Companies and institutions will desperately need humans who can do the things that the new tools can’t.

You are not behind.

You are early if you move now.

The time to develop specific knowledge is before everyone agrees it’s valuable. Right now, there’s still room to go deep and wide on something. And become genuinely good at it before the field gets crowded. The best version of this process, developing specific knowledge, going deep, building something valuable, isn’t just a career shot. It’s a way of living that has value beyond tech disruption. It’s also how you stay ahead of the anxiety of trying to stay relevant without ever committing to anything. Follow your curiosity even when it’s not immediately practical. Built expertise that reflects practical investment of time and attention.

The 9–5 system asks for your time and intelligence. The new model is more uncertain, demanding, and personal. But also more rewarding over the long-term. Don’t make reaction (waiting to see what happens, hoping to stay relevant long enough) a way of life. Be more proactive by deciding now what you’re going to become the best version of.

Your starting point is to write down the three or four problems you find genuinely interesting. Things you consistently have opinions, questions or ideas that go beyond common knowledge. Now, is there a version of you that goes five years into it without losing your mind What would that person know What could they do that most people couldn’t Start going there, deliberately. The 9–5 was a 20th-century solution. It worked while systems supported it. It’s now a 21st-century crisis.

Nobody has your corner more than you.

Save yourself while you still have time.
