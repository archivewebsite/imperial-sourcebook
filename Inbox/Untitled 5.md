**Don’t make big decisions when you’re extremely happy or extremely angry. Strong emotions can make your judgment feel crystal clear, which is exactly when it may be least reliable. Pause, cool down, and revisit the decision when your mind is steadier. Your future self deserves better than being governed by your temporary emotional weather.**

Research backs this up. A major review in _Annual Review of Psychology_ found that emotions are “potent, pervasive, predictable” drivers of judgment and choice, sometimes helpful but sometimes harmful, because they can change what we notice, how we estimate risk, and what options feel reasonable. ([Annual Reviews](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-psych-010213-115043/cite/medline?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Annual Reviews"))

Anger is especially risky for decision-making. Lerner and Keltner’s research found that angry people tend to make more optimistic risk estimates and more risk-seeking choices, while fear pushes people in the opposite direction. In other words, anger can make bad ideas look weirdly executable, because apparently the human brain enjoys adding gasoline to paperwork. ([Greater Good](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/dacherkeltner/docs/lerner.fear.jpsp.2001.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Fear, Anger, and Risk - Greater Good"))

Extreme happiness can also distort judgment, not because joy is bad, but because high optimism can make people focus more on positive outcomes and underweight possible downsides. Research on optimism and risk-taking shows that optimistic people tend to focus more on favorable outcomes, which can increase willingness to take risks. ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11166-023-09409-z?utm_source=chatgpt.com "On the psychology of the relation between optimism and risk taking"))

A practical rule: **for decisions with real consequences, wait until the emotion drops from “I must act now” to “I can think clearly.”** Then write down the options, risks, benefits, and what you would advise a friend to do. That tiny delay is often the difference between wisdom and a dramatic little disaster wearing confidence as a hat.
