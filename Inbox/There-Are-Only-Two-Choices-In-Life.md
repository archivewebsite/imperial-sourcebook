## The first is about acceptance. The second is about responsibility.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*3TtJQt1tFtB7j2IW)

Photo by Aliaksei Lepik on Unsplash

In 2017, I was told a story I’ll never forget.

It was the first day of a tantra retreat, and I had signed up because I wanted to learn how to forgive my past and love myself. It was a daunting task, and one that I didn’t know was possible. But at that point, I was desperate.

After we’d all gathered in a circle, I was expecting the teacher to introduce himself or tell us how the 4-day retreat would go. He didn’t.

Instead, he started with a story that set the tone for what was to come.

> *A young boy was playing in a field when a butterfly landed in the middle of his palm.*
> 
> *This butterfly was so beautiful that it made the young boy want to keep it all to himself. So he quickly closed his fist and held it tight.*
> 
> *When he opened it again, he realised he had accidentally crushed the multi-coloured, paper-thin being. The butterfly lay limp in his hand.*
> 
> *The young boy was distraught. He couldn’t believe it. He was so upset with what he had done that he walked home and went straight to bed.*
> 
> *As luck would have it, while he was playing in the same field the very next day, another butterfly flew into his hand.*
> 
> *This time, even though he wanted to keep its beauty all to himself again, he knew he must keep his palm open and enjoy the delicate little creature for as long as it stayed, even if that meant it might fly off at any moment.*
> 
> *That day, the young boy learned a valuable lesson:* ==*To love something, he must give it freedom. For love to return, he must let it go.*==
> 
> *That’s what we’re going to be doing together this week. We’re going to learn how to live with an open palm so we can love more freely.*

Those five days changed my life in so many ways, but something that’s stayed with me ever since is that I can only live with an open palm if I love myself first. If I don’t, I will close my fist and trap love because it fills a hole.

Sadly, I’ve done that too many times in the past, and each relationship suffocated as a result.

Later that day, my teacher asked the group a question: “Can you accept and love yourself for who and what you are, or can you be brave enough to change?”

Both sounded as daunting as the other, but I decided to give myself fully over to process.

I learned to stop focusing on the things that were bad, wrong, or needed fixing in my life, and instead, I searched for all the things that were good, right, and already working.

I had to be very honest with myself and admit that I was (and still am) a flawed human being. ==So in order for me to love myself, I had to accept all the things that were imperfect.==

That was incredibly confronting and uncomfortable because for so many years, I was on a crusade to fix them, not accept them. However, this only reinforced my belief that I needed fixing, which was causing a lot of anxiety and feelings of unworthiness.

When I finally accepted my flaws, I found there was *more* freedom to change and grow because there wasn’t a self-critical voice in my head waiting to annihilate me whenever I make a mistake.

That voice used to be so cruel, but when it was gone, I could look at myself with more curiosity and take more risks.

“Love is inherently risky, especially self-love, but the alternative is worse.” My teacher said. “Everything is about loving or not loving in the end.”

When I was given that simple choice, the answer was obvious.

Take the risk.

==Choose love.==
