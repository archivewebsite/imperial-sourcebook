Democratizing access to artificial intelligence

## Four years of obsessive tracking, gastritis, and a single realization that keeps the brain fry from relapsing.

I started researching AI for two reasons.

First, I was worried that not understanding it would make me unable to hold my ground in the workplace.

Second, I’m not getting any younger. It already felt rough four years ago, and things are only going to get harder; so I figured that if I didn’t pick up something new, I’d end up graduating early from the workforce.

Tools, large language models, skills, you name it, I tracked every single one.

Every new update, I’d check it out and try it. I became obsessed, barely sleeping, worried about *what if I missed anything* in my analysis, and feeling like I’d lost my mind.

And **then my body started sending warning signals, gastritis, for which a GP** couldn’t find a cause. Finally recovered after three weeks.

It reminded me of all those sudden-death cases I’d seen. I know how this might sound extreme, but from all the data I’ve seen so far, I am not the only one feeling this burnout.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:2000/format:webp/1*_folyjfv6hmmwfZKgiHPGA.png)

## AI Brain Fry

Here’s the latest survey data by [HBR](https://hbr.org/2026/03/when-using-ai-leads-to-brain-fry). They surveyed ~1,500 US workers in every industry, and here’s the summary chart:

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*2jDQTA6NzWlcvH0U.png)

==Just maybe you didn’t pay attention and were too busy to take it seriously, but that doesn’t mean you aren’t stressed by all the AI slops, the hypes, the push to do more with less.== Or maybe you’d thought the hardship was temporary, that you’d push through and be fine.

> AI brain fry doesn’t come with a badge. It’s just burnout with better branding.

Using GenAI to chase a better image or code works the same way as swiping on TikTok or pulling a lever of a slot machine gets you hooked. You prompt, re-roll, tweak, re-roll.

Three of the four generations are cursed; one’s almost right, so you pull the lever again.

I also think AI addiction burnout is worse because it wears a productivity lanyard,so the re-prompting feels like iterating; also, the *image in my head* is fuzzy and subjective, so the next spin is always closer; GenAI costs time *and* credits, so the sunk cost makes quitting harder, not easier.

Between the physical warnings and the burnout stories I’ve heard from friends, I thought it’s about time to make an effort to slow down — now, after four years of chasing it.

At the same time, the more I learned about AI, the more immune I became to product demos and new releases.

Here’s how I’ve come to see it.

## First: Learning how to use AI is pointless.

Because models update so fast that whatever you learn becomes outdated every few months.

==The goal of AI development is to eventually let anyone complete tasks through natural language. So any barriers to using AI will be flattened in the next iteration.==

Teaching others how to vibe code, or using AI to generate movies or images, is an even more hopeless endeavor. The technique expires when the tool updates. ==***What doesn’t expire is knowing what good engineering looks like, what makes a design work, what a well-structured argument reads like***====.==

Those are the things that equip you to best evaluate whatever comes next — including the AI output sitting in front of you.

[Researchers tested this in 2026](https://idibell.cat/en/2026/03/human-creativity-still-surpasses-creative-generative-ai-according-to-recent-research/). They trained a model on visual artists’ own drawings, then had both produce work. The artists still won on originality, liveliness, and aesthetics.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*hdoL2idCnQrjdCKf.png)

==The model had their style. It didn’t have their perception.==

Without that intuition, you’re just learning to operate a tool that will look different in six months.

With this in mind, there’s no point in just learning the ways of manipulating GenAI. The time you spend studying AI workflows would be better spent reading a book or learning a fundamental theory.

A hundred times more useful to your future self.

## Second: Using AI to do your work is also pointless.

You spend enormous effort getting your AI to do a task, at least I did.

Setting up folders, rules, skills, and you finally get a workflow running that produces the output you want. Then the next model update absorbs all that effort (and knowledge), and now anyone can get the same result with a single sentence.

If you are an early adopter, simply think back to what GPTs you were chasing in 2023, and now the skill or the OpenClaw you lost sleep over. Everything you did was, in effect, replaced by the next generation; **the most value you provide is creating *high-quality training material* for the model.**

And before you’ve even ==had a chance to enjoy the edge a finely tuned AI agent (workflow, skill) gave you, that gap between you and everyone else gets wiped out soon enough.==

It’s like colleagues who never bother researching Claude or Cursor, whose AI knowledge extends to occasionally reading a headline; they plod along doing their work the old way, and the time you saved using Claude, plus whatever extra thinking you did, amounts to nothing distinguishable in front of management.

## What’s left is to do things the hard way.

There is one version of engaging with AI that’s worth something, and it looks nothing like either of those.

Stop jumping right into a new tool and learn it. Stop building skills or workflows around it.

Using it enough — *deliberately, periodically* — to know where it hits its ceiling. That ceiling is the thing nobody talks about in any fancy demos. And it’s the only thing worth understanding.

Part of learning that ceiling is ***noticing when you’re reaching for AI***, not because you need it, but because sitting with a hard problem feels uncomfortable.

I started catching myself opening a chat the moment something got difficult. The prompt was a way to avoid thinking. When I waited, let’s say, five minutes, I usually already knew what I wanted to say.

The AI would have just handed it back to me in more words.

Brainstorming with AI has the exact same problem. Arguably, brainstorming is the one thing I hear most often about what people do with AI, so I’ll use it as an example.

==Every idea AI generates is already everywhere on the internet. It gives you the most statistically likely version of an idea.== That’s the last thing you need when you’re trying to find the angle nobody’s taken.

[Researchers ran the numbers on this properly](https://hbr.org/2026/03/researchers-asked-llms-for-strategic-advice-they-got-trendslop-in-return). Using seven major LLMs — ChatGPT, Claude, DeepSeek, Gemini, Grok, Mistral, GPT-5 — tested thousands of times on real strategic questions, across industries, with prompts reworded and reframed every which way.

The result was the same every time: differentiate, collaborate, think long-term, innovate, and centralize. As you can see in the image below:

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1100/format:webp/0*oPNFspMWW6W7niXC.png)

==You don’t need 15,000 trials (as the researchers did) to see it. You just need to notice when you’re getting back the internet’s average opinion.==

The models consistently converged on the same buzzy, fashionable recommendations regardless of which industry context or prompt variation you provided.

Apart from brainstorming, I imagine some of you are probably amazed by how GPT-Image2 has now surpassed the capabilities of nano banana.

I spent a few hours with GPT-Image 2. It can get close to what you’re picturing, but only once your instructions are specific enough that you’ve essentially already done the creative work yourself. By the time I figured that out, I’d spent more time than if I’d just found a reference image and briefed someone directly.

Both are scenarios worth noticing (you might find your own moments). Neither required you to become an expert.

The version of AI engagement that earns its place looks like this:

1. Try AI when it’s new enough to matter, learn the actual trade-offs,
2. Then decide deliberately whether it belongs in your work. That’s different from chasing every release. It’s also considerably cheaper on your nervous system.
3. ***Prompt less, think more.***

## When the AI’s wild goose chasing phase is over.

The models will keep improving. The releases won’t stop.

But I kept noticing something once I started using these tools seriously: ==AI made me faster at producing things; what it couldn’t do was tell me== ==*which things were worth producing.*==

==And I often end up throwing away most of what AI produces.==

Which is why the most important thing — has been and always will be — is ***our judgment:*** ***knowing what to pursue, what to finish, what to abandon***. Judgment will always be the bottleneck. As I mentioned in my article about [distilling your colleague](https://www.2ndorderthinkers.com/p/distill-the-story-of-an-ex-colleague?r=2x3l2g&utm_campaign=post&utm_medium=web).

Judgment is the ceiling that the next release won’t close.

Not because the AI companies aren’t trying. **Because it’s not an engineering problem.**

Once you see this, the new releases stop landing the same way. Instead, something drops, you read about it, you understand roughly what it does, and you go back to whatever you were actually thinking about.

This can be a cliché, but ==the question was never which tool is fastest.== ==***It was always what you’re trying to do with it.***==
