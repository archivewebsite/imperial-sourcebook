## Overhyped, underrated, or both? A linguist’s verdict from the evidence.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*uEd_XuPNQHpl9Mv_)

Photo by ilgmyzin on Unsplash

Last summer, my country-house neighbor, a man of 78, told me he was moving to Spain.

He wanted to learn Spanish before the move. He told me this with the slightly proud air of someone who had already taken the first practical step.

“I’ve started,” he said. “I downloaded Duolingo.”

Of course he did.

For a seventy-eight-year-old retired man in rural Sweden who has never previously thought about second-language acquisition, Duolingo is not one option among many. It is the only word that comes to mind when you say “language learning.”

Same for my SFI (Swedish for Immigrants) students, for my friends starting Italian at 40, and for the parents in my daughter’s class who want their kids to pick up a third language.

It seems to have become a universal choice, the first and best method for learning a language.

For most of the world right now, learning a language equals downloading Duolingo. It is not one of the choices. It is the whole category.

Which makes the questions I keep getting more important than it sounds.

*Is Duolingo any good?*

*What do you think about Duolingo?*

I have spent 20 years teaching languages, and I have my opinions about apps, but my opinions are not why my readers are here.

I am a scientist with a PhD in Linguistics. And a scientist’s job is not to take sides. It is to look at the evidence and tell you what is there. So I went deep into the research, the controlled studies, the longitudinal ones, the comparative ones, the systematic reviews, the ones funded by Duolingo, and the ones independent of them.

I wrote it up as a meta-review. Don’t worry, this is not a boring academic review in incomprehensible language. I made it easy to read and digest (hopefully).

By the end of this piece, you will know what Duolingo actually does, what it cannot do, why some people swear by it, and others quit in fury, and what to do with all of that if you are sitting on the sofa with your phone, wondering whether to start.

I will also tell you about the cat. The cat is important.

## What Duolingo actually is

The numbers are cartoonishly large.

As of late 2025, Duolingo had 133 million monthly users and 52 million daily users (Duolingo Inc., 2026).

More than five million users currently hold streaks longer than a year, and the longest recorded streak passes four thousand days, which is to say, eleven years of unbroken daily practice (SQ Magazine, 2026).

The green owl sends roughly 1.5 billion notifications a day, more than the entire population of China (Gitnux, 2026).

The app is free. The barrier to entry is the time it takes to install something on your phone. The whole experience is engineered with surgical care to feel like a game, not like school: points, leagues, confetti when you finish a lesson, a heartbeat-style streak counter that turns red when you have not opened the app in 20 hours.

This is not language learning the way your high-school teacher remembers it. This is language learning the way a video-game studio designs a slot machine. I do not mean that as an insult. I mean it as a fact, and the fact matters, because the retention engineering is what makes Duolingo the largest language-learning product in human history. The retention engineering is also where some of the trouble lives. We will get there.

One number, though, almost nobody mentions. The vast majority of people who download the app never finish a course. Most never finish the first week. The completion rate for the full curriculum is, by industry-standard estimates, in the low single digits. Hold that thought.

## My own time with the owl

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*decbgWMEcs-3kdMU8FEwWQ.png)

A screenshot from my Duolingo profile

Years ago, before any of the recent studies were published and before the green owl had become a meme, I tried Duolingo myself. I was not their best customer, as you can see from the screenshot.

The first lessons were satisfying in the way the early days of any course are satisfying: every minute fills in a blank space. ==I built a streak==. I climbed leagues. I felt the small dopamine spark every time the screen flashed green.

Somewhere in the second or third week, the loop began.

*The cat drinks milk.*

*The cat drinks the milk.*

*The cat drinks milk every morning.*

==I had already learned the verb== ==*to drink.*== ==And there I was, on my way to work, being asked once again whether the cat was, in fact, drinking the milk.==

Reader, I quit.

I felt slightly guilty about the streak.

I did not quit because the app was bad. I quit because the app was not designed for me, and because the streak had begun to feel like a small ==obligation== rather than a small joy.

I was practicing the *opening of the app* more than I was practicing the language. Those are two different activities, and the difference matters more than I realized at the time.

## What the evidence actually shows

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*SgwWGrkHbJ6EcaL_)

Photo by Houcine Ncib on Unsplash

Rather than march you through every study, I have organized the findings around the questions a real person asks when they are deciding whether to subscribe to the premium tier.

## ‘Will I actually learn anything?‘

Yes. This is the part that marketing has the data for.

The clearest evidence comes from a three-month pretest-posttest study by Smith, Jiang, and Peters (2024). After roughly 27 hours of practice, participants improved in reading, vocabulary, grammar, ==and even speaking==, with effect sizes the researchers describe as moderate to large, indicating the gains were real.

Two larger studies with a sample of 225 and 245 learners confirmed the pattern: about 100 hours of practice reliably moves adults to lower intermediate or solid intermediate levels in receptive skills (Jiang et al., 2021, 2024).

In plain language, the app will move you forward. You will start recognizing words on signs, in song lyrics, and in film subtitles. You will read children’s books in your target language and understand most of them. The first hundred hours are honestly invested.

But notice the size of that promise.

*Receptive skills (listening and reading).*

==Not fluency, free conversation, or productive writing.==

The research is honest about this in the methods.

The marketing, less so.

## ‘Why does my listening lag so far behind my reading?’

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*kaA5916Ubexe3QoS)

Photo by Johnny Briggs on Unsplash

Because that is what apps do.

Reading climbs faster than listening in nearly every study that tested both, across multiple languages and learner populations (Smith et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2021; González-Fernández & de la Viña, 2025; Kim et al., 2025).

The one exception worth flagging is Jiang et al. (2024), where listening caught up to reading at the end of A2 in three English courses, although that study had no control group and no measurement of informal exposure to English (movies, work, social media), which the authors openly flag as a limitation.

So, why does listening tend to lag?

When you read a sentence, you control the speed. The text waits.

When you listen to a native speaker, they do not wait. They drop syllables, run words together, use slang you have not been taught, and sneeze halfway through the sentence, so you have to recover meaning from context.

Duolingo’s audio is slow, clean, and predictable. It is the still-pond version.

Real speech is the river.

Some skills transfer, kicking and breathing, but the moment you step into the actual current, you discover what the pond did not teach you.

## ‘Is it really “as good as four semesters of university”?’

This is the most frequently repeated marketing claim, and the methods do not support it.

The headline rests on Jiang et al. (2021), who tested 225 adult Duolingo learners of Spanish and French on the ACTFL Reading and Listening Proficiency Tests. The study compared their scores against published university data from fourth-semester students (Winke et al., 2020) and found that the Duolingo learners performed comparably.

End of headline.

Read the methodology, though, and the comparison falls apart.

==The Duolingo learners had an average age of 44. The university students were 19 to 22 years old==.

The Duolingo cohort was 90% college-educated, 14% with doctorates, all self-selected through a recruitment email and paid $100 to participate.

The university students were taking a required course that they did not want to take.

The Duolingo learners had self-paced learning over a median of 562 days in a country saturated with Spanish-language media and were asked, by self-report alone, whether they had used “only Duolingo.”

Nobody verified, because nobody could. There was no random assignment, no pretest, and no control group within the study itself.

Reframe the same data, and the headline collapses.

Forty-four-year-olds with doctorates score similarly on a Spanish reading test to unmotivated nineteen-year-olds in a course they cannot stand.

Of course they do.

Birdsong (2018) has documented over the past decades that adults bring substantial metalinguistic advantages to receptive tasks.

The four-semester claim is not a lie. It is also not the whole truth.

## ‘Why does the streak feel so important to me?’

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*Ys5o-wop5jqhHzPs)

Photo by Quino Al on Unsplash

==Because the streak is what the app is actually selling==.

Sudina and Plonsky (2024) ran a natural experiment with hundreds of Duolingo learners, using actual in-app behavioral data (sessions, hours, intensity) rather than self-report, and asked which usage patterns predicted persistence and proficiency.

The findings show that *frequency* and *intensity* of practice mattered more than total hours, and what predicted long-term persistence in the first place was not language ability but *motivation,* *age,* and *L2 grit,* the disposition to keep going on language tasks specifically.

In other words, the streak does not build the learner.

The learner builds the streak.

This is the dragon at the center of the Duolingo research, and almost nobody talks about it out loud.

Every study reporting positive findings recruits learners who have completed hundreds of hours of practice. They are survivors. The vast majority of people who download the app are not these people, and they have not been studied.

Asking *"Does Duolingo work?*" by studying only the people who finished is methodologically equivalent to asking *"Does jogging make you fit?*" by surveying only marathon finishers.

## ‘Will Duolingo replace a teacher?’

No. The research is unusually clear on this.

Three studies have tested Duolingo head-to-head against classroom instruction, and they tell a consistent story (Rachels & Rockinson-Szapkiw, 2018; Kim et al., 2025; González-Fernández & de la Viña, 2025).

Duolingo wins on vocabulary and reading recognition.

==Classrooms win in listening comprehension and complex use==.

The combined groups, learners using both an app and a classroom, often beat either method alone.

The pattern makes intuitive sense.

The app is excellent at low-stakes repetition: thousands of small chances to recognize a word, on your phone, on your couch, at midnight, with no human to disappoint.

The classroom does what the app cannot: real-time correction, social pressure that forces you to retrieve from memory rather than recognize from a list, the small embarrassment that pushes you to improve, and the cultural joke you would never have learned on your own.

They are not in competition. They are doing different jobs.

## ‘What about the new AI conversation features?’

This is where the research gets interesting, and where it asks for caution.

Duolingo’s own scientists report that learners using the new GPT-4-powered Roleplay and Explain My Answer features feel significantly more capable of using the language in real situations after one month of use (Kittredge et al., 2025).

The study was conducted by the company that makes the product and has no independent control group, so read the results as suggestive rather than conclusive.

Stronger evidence on anxiety reduction comes from an independent study of sixty Chinese students preparing for the IELTS speaking test, where AI conversation practice substantially reduced speaking anxiety, with the largest effects in the most anxious learners (Ding & Yusof, 2025).

Compare both with standard Duolingo (without conversational AI), which yields gains in speaking and self-regulation but no significant reduction in anxiety (Qiao & Zhao, 2023).

The pattern: gamified practice motivates you.

AI conversation practice changes how you feel about speaking.

The reason is the difference between *feeling* capable and *being* capable.

Bandura (1997) showed that the strongest source of self-belief is mastery experience, the act of doing the difficult thing and getting through it. A point and a badge give you the feeling of mastery. A conversation, even with an AI, gives you something closer to the substance.

But there is a warning attached.

A controlled field experiment (Bastani et al., 2025) with nearly a thousand high-school mathematics students in Turkey compared three groups:

1. practicing with standard ChatGPT,
2. practicing with a custom GPT tutor that had pedagogical guardrails, and
3. practicing with no AI at all.

The standard ChatGPT group performed *better* during practice.

They felt as though they were learning faster.

Then the researchers administered a final exam without access to AI. The standard ChatGPT group scored 17% worse than the no-AI control.

The custom-tutor group, with the guardrails, performed about the same as the no-AI group.

The tool can make tasks feel easier while quietly hollowing out the underlying skills required to do them without it.

## ‘What is missing from the research?’

A great deal.

A systematic review of the published Duolingo literature found that the field has focused on engagement, retention, and short-term receptive proficiency, while remaining largely silent on long-term outcomes, productive skills, and real-world communicative transfer (Shortt et al., 2023).

We know the app builds recognition.

We do not yet know, in the rigorous sense, whether it builds the ability to *do* something with that recognition outside the app, in a real conversation, in writing meant for a real reader, five years from now.

Until those studies exist, treat any “Duolingo will make you fluent” claim with the same skepticism you would treat a diet ad.

## The Verdict

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*ylKqY-jnrr1coXVG)

Photo by Tingey Injury Law Firm on Unsplash

If you skipped the studies and jumped straight here, welcome.

This section is for you. The whole point of the meta-review was to land at this set of conclusions, each one tied to the evidence I read.

Duolingo is not amazing. It is also not useless.

The honest reading of the science sits in a more interesting place than either side of the internet wants to admit, and it leads to ten concrete decisions you can make.

## 1\. Use Duolingo for the first hundred hours. The early gains are real.

This is the part that the marketing actually has the data for.

A motivated adult will move from zero to lower-intermediate in receptive skills with around 100 hours of practice, sometimes more (Smith et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2021, 2024).

The barrier to entry is the time it takes to install something on your phone; the early dopamine of a flashing green screen will carry you through the dull bits, and you will start recognizing words on signs, in subtitles, in song lyrics.

The first hundred hours are honestly invested.

This is the reason I tell my SFI students to download the app. This is why I would tell my countryhouse neighbor to keep it on his phone for a short time, then move on to more serious and varied practice, either alone or with an expert, or enroll in a language course.

## 2\. Do not believe the four-semester comparison.

You are not getting two years of university Spanish from your phone.

The headline study compared self-selected, college-educated forty-four-year-olds to nineteen-year-olds in a required course they did not want to take, without random assignment or a pretest (Jiang et al., 2021).

What you are actually getting is a hundred hours of focused receptive practice, which is real but bounded. Adjust your expectations accordingly.

Anyone telling you otherwise is selling, not teaching.

## 3\. Leave the app the moment the streak becomes the practice.

This is the part that happened to me with the cat.

If you find yourself doing the same exercises about basic verbs for the fourth week in a row, the streak has become the practice, and the practice has stopped being the language.

Persistence builds streaks, not the other way around.

The app’s retention engineering exists because Duolingo is a public company that needs daily active users to grow.

The honest test: if you cannot remember the last time a Duolingo lesson taught you something you did not already know, you have outgrown it.

Move on.

Read a book. Have a real conversation. Watch a film without subtitles. The app is a runway, not a destination.

## 4\. Use the new AI conversation features if speaking anxiety is your barrier, but never as a substitute for real practice.

The evidence is clearest for these features as low-stakes rehearsal spaces.

They are bridges to real interaction, not substitutes for it. Use them to build the procedural confidence you need before facing a human partner. Then face the human partner.

But resist the temptation to lean on the hint button. Every time the AI finishes your sentence for you, you are losing the retrieval practice that builds actual fluency.

## 5\. The teacher is not optional. Apps and humans do different jobs.

The classroom outperforms the app on listening comprehension and complex use, and combined groups consistently beat either method alone.

The app is excellent at vocabulary and reading recognition. The classroom is excellent at the messy, real-time correction that builds fluency.

They do different jobs.

If you want to actually speak and understand at speed, you will need a teacher, a tutor, a conversation partner, or a community at some point. Plan for it. Schedule it. Pay for it if needed.

## 6\. Plan for the listening lag.

Reading outpaces listening in most studies, and apps cannot fix this on their own.

Duolingo’s audio is slow, clean, and predictable. Real native speech is none of those things.

Do not wait until your reading is solid to start serious listening practice. Watch films in the target language with subtitles in the target language, then without. Listen to podcasts at a slower speed first, then at native speed. Get used to not understanding everything and recovering meaning from context.

Your listening will catch up only if you train it directly. The app will not do this for you.

## 7\. The research has not yet measured the things that matter most. Read accordingly.

We know the app builds recognition.

We do not know whether it will be able to write a real letter, hold a real conversation, give a real presentation, or use the language with confidence five years from now.

Speaking is barely measured anywhere.

Writing is essentially absent.

Long-term retention is unstudied (Shortt et al., 2023).

Until those studies exist, treat any “Duolingo will make you fluent” claim with skepticism. Science has not caught up to marketing yet, and may not for years.

## 8\. Stack your tools. No single app does everything well.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*IcUqD2ANUCp0JlWx)

Photo by Or Hakim on Unsplash

Duolingo is excellent for vocabulary recognition.

It is weaker in pronunciation, where Memrise tends to perform better (Pichugin et al., 2023), and there is no clear winner over ChatGPT-based instruction in motivation, autonomy, or critical thinking (Xu & Liu, 2025).

The polyglot internet loves arguing about which app is best, but the question is framed incorrectly.

Treat your language learning the way a chef treats a kitchen. You do not pick one knife. You pick the right knife for the cut.

Duolingo for vocabulary recognition. Anki or Memrise for harder spaced repetition. Pimsleur or Glossika for listening. A real tutor on iTalki or Preply for speaking. The question is not which app to use. It is the combination to build.

## 9\. Track sessions and accuracy, not minutes.

The hours you spend in the app do not predict what you learn.

The number of completed sessions and your accuracy rate inside them do (Smith et al., 2024; Sudina & Plonsky, 2024).

A focused twenty-minute session at high accuracy beats an hour of distracted scrolling.

A thirty-minute session where you sleepwalk through five lessons at sixty per cent accuracy is doing less for your fluency than a fifteen-minute session where you complete three lessons at ninety per cent.

Stop measuring your practice in time spent.

Start measuring it in lessons completed and answers retrieved correctly. If a lesson did not stick, redo it.

## 10\. Use Duolingo to build the meta-skill that outlasts the app.

While you are using it for the first 100 hours, pay attention to how you are using it.

Duolingo can help build self-regulated learning skills, the broader capacity to manage your own study, set goals, monitor progress, and adjust strategies (Fathi et al., 2024; Yang & Ying, 2026).

The best language learners I have taught in twenty years all share one trait.

It is the meta-skill of knowing how they learn, what they need to work on, and how to make themselves do the work, even on days when they do not feel like it.

That skill transfers to every language they will ever study, and to most domains beyond language.

Notice when you focus and when you drift. Notice which strategies produce real retrieval and which produce only a streak.

The vocabulary you learn from the app may fade. The meta-skill of running your own learning will not.

==The cat drinks milk. You already knew that. It is time for something else==.
