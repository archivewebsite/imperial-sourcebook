## Life refined my values, and this is the result

![A confident woman in her 50s smiles](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*BsIushmPuMF9QIQg)

Photo by Artem Beliaikin on Unsplash

When diligent, ==people mature like umm, fine wine== (I was going to say cheese) as they age, and I’m no exception.

Life lessons taught me to stop doing these ==10 things that worsen my wellbeing, peace of mind, and relationships.== I don’t do them now and my life is all the better for it.

### 1: Try to control the uncontrollable

If we could calculate how long we spend worrying about things we can’t change, I wonder what the answer would be? I’m guessing it’s as least as long as we spend in line. We can’t get a figure for worrying, but let’s check queuing.

Do make a cup of coffee while I Google results.

Here we go.

According to [National News](https://nationalnews.co.uk/news/wait-in-line-nearly-a-year-of-our-life-is-spent-in-queues-24514/), “The average adult will spend the equivalent of almost one year of their lives stuck in a queue, it has been revealed.” The results refer to Brits, of course. Some cultures might not be as patient.

The facts continue, “During the average adult lifetime of 60.5 years this is a staggering 273 days, or 6,554 hours spent queuing.”

(Yes, I know. They state the average lifetime is 60.5 years. That’s a bit pessimistic of you ask me.)

Of course, comparing time spent worrying with standing in line may be unreliable. But anyway, the point is we waste enough time doing menial things already without trying to control uncontrollable events.

==I roll my eyes looking back at the times I worried about everything from how my career choices would turn out to events I struggled with because they weren’t going as planned.== If I had queued instead, at least I may have been rewarded with an ice cream or entrance to the theatre. Worrying got me nowhere.

==Now in my 50s, I’m more accepting, having learned that sometimes (not always) things really do turn out for the best. Outcomes I didn’t consider or think I wanted served me well, after all.==

These days, I get through tough times more easily as I’m less stressed, even if they don’t end up as I like.

### 2: Worry about what people think of me

I can’t begin to explain what a relief it is not to care as much about what people think of me.

Phew!

==Life showed me that no one cares as much as you think about what you do, your appearance, or anything else.== Mostly, they are busy thinking about themselves and what to do next. They are too preoccupied to waste time thinking about your antics.

A fleeting thought that someone might not like what I say may pop to mind, but it’s soon replaced by peace. I don’t need people to like me, which is terrific. It means I can be myself without bending my ways to meet others’ tastes and preferences.

### 3: Think/act unkindly toward my body

Letter to my body that says it all:

***Dear body, I’m sorry for not appreciating you in the past and for overlooking your brilliance. Even when I was a dress size bigger than I wanted to be, you did all the fantastic things you do automatically to keep me going. You didn’t take umbrage when I chastised you for not being slim.***

***You never complained when I stuffed bits of you into tight jeans and uncomfortable shoes. Not only that, but you didn’t forsake me when I ate unhealthy junk foods (although the word “food” here is debatable).***

==***You’re a great pal, and I’m glad I finally recognize this. Now, I treat you with the respect you deserve and am forever grateful.***==

***Thank you, love from Bridget.***

### 4: Struggle to be right

It took me years to recognize being wrong didn’t matter. And I thought everyone should acknowledge those times when I was correct. Struggling to get people to see my side of every story, tell me I was right, and confess they were wrong was painful. It felt like a crusade.

I’m happy to say time showed me that whether people agreed with me made no odds. Letting go of striving felt like setting down heavy baggage. I’m lighter now. I happily let people have their say when I silently disagree with them. After all, who cares? Not the woman I am today.

And if they don’t see my side of a story? I don’t worry.

Letting go of the need to be right taught me to listen to others’ views and be curious about them. Sometimes, I learn something new. Or discovering I’m wrong and another person’s right feels like a win. At least I know more than I did before.

### 5: Compare what I’ve got to what others have

I like to think I’ve never been one for trying to keep up with those who have it all. But I admit that I often compared everything from my appearance to earning power to others.

We do this naturally as a way of discovering whether we’re up-to-spec. But comparison has a downside. It’s so inaccurate.

We can’t accurately compare ourselves because we’re so different. Our talents, circumstances, and backgrounds vary. Many things play a part in where we are in life.

As a happy person over 50, I know comparison is a trap. I appreciate what I have instead of focusing on what I don’t have. And I don’t see myself or others as not good enough. Life taught me that the things I thought worth striving for weren’t accurate, dependable measures of success.

==Success, for me, is love and happiness, and if I can be healthy and comfortable financially too, terrific!==

### 6: Worry about impressing people

As young folks, we were taught we would face various events, where making a good impression was essential. Everything from job interviews to meeting our partner’s parents were opportunities to look bad, or, if we got it right, shine.

Now, I am me, *as is*. If I’m a match for your company or circle of friends, you’ll see we’re compatible. If not, I wouldn’t want to pretend otherwise, anyway.

Occasionally, someone dislikes my taste or views, and that’s fine. We differ, and this makes the world more interesting. If you’re unimpressed with me, so what. It just means you approach things from other angles, and we might learn from each other.

### 7: Regret things I’ve done in the past

It made sense to have regrets. After all, you’ve learned nothing from unhappy experiences if you would repeat them given a chance. But is that true?

Not always.

You wouldn’t be who you are today or be as wise if you hadn’t made mistakes and poor choices before.

==Life has showed me that many of the harshest challenges from the past led to huge boosts in life improvement. I changed for the better because of them.==

### 8: Use food and drink as rewards

If you asked me in my 40s, I’d have said I didn’t use food and drinks as rewards much. But I now know this isn’t so. Like many folks, I was raised in a home where we rewarded ourselves with scrumptious treats when we’d done well.

==But we did the same when we were sad and felt let down, and when we faced difficulties. Sweets, snacks, and even gin and tonics, once I was old enough, were plasters or painkillers when things seemed to be going wrong and when they went especially right.==

As a happy person in my 50s, I no longer use food and drink like that.

It was a hard habit to break. At first, the urge to indulge was strong. But I discovered using food and drink as crutches was unhelpful. When I stopped eating as many biscuits, sweets, and other treats, and quit having an evening drink just because I could, many brilliant changes happened.

I lost weight, my gut health improved, and my skin became clear. I gained energy, had less brain fog, and the onset of what looked like arthritis vanished. The swelling around a bone spur on my foot went, leaving me able to wear my favourite boots again, and I can wear my beautiful dress I hadn’t previously fitted into since 2005.

In case you’re wondering, I didn’t go on a diet. I had a heart-to-heart with myself and decided I loved me enough to treat myself with respect. That statement makes it sound easy. But I assure you, it wasn’t! The agreement with myself was just the start of an ongoing journey. I used tough love like an unrelenting coach.

### 9: Take my blessings for granted

==As I mature, I recognize my blessings. I didn’t always pay attention to them as a kid. You face loss as you age. It’s part of the deal when you enter life. Nothing is permanent. Everything shifts, and the only thing you can really maintain is your mindset.==

Each night I run through all the things I appreciate. I start with my snuggly bed and move through the house with my mind to uncover other things I love. My husband, the dog, my courtyard… And further afield, relatives, friends.

### 10: Consume haphazardly

==Years ago, I loved collecting stuff. Now I love decluttering and refining my environment down to what I need and find wonderful. Anything else has no place in my home.==

Most of us are consumers in many ways. We consume social media, movies, and food. We take in vast amounts of data, so much that we get stressed by all the choices or bombardment of bad news.

As a positive person over 50, I am particular about what I consume, knowing it matters. What goes in (whether in your head, heart, or stomach) affects your life. It accumulates and becomes part of you.

The same is true about your habits, how you treat people, and the stories you tell yourself about what matters.

I’m more content these days, knowing I pressure myself less, and others, to behave in certain ways. I don’t mind waiting and seeing what happens, and I know what makes me happy.
