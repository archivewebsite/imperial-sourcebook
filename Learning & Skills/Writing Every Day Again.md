# Writing Every Day Again

## Inside a self-created writing challenge using mindset rewiring, smart habits, and practical strategies to stay consistent for good

Image of the author

Getting back to an old habit you’ve practiced for years is not that simple. I’ve been failing to get back to writing for quite some time now.

I aimed to publish once a day on Medium. Sounds challenging, but I used to do it with a full-time job a few years back, like no biggie. Everything came to a halt when I got married and moved to an island.

[Moving abroad](https://www.youtube.com/watchv=QlXQ3cn685Y) disrupted my creative rhythm that was once part of my everyday routine. Losing an integral part of myself also hampered my confidence. I could no longer multitask or manage multiple projects.

This wasn’t the first time I was in a creative rut, but it was the longest.

If I don’t act, I may never get out of this phase. So I got to work.

## The plan to get back to the game

Writing is a game. The more you play, the more you want to play.

- The first piece of writing made me question myself.
- The second one asked me if I seriously wanna do it.
- The third one gave me a dopamine hit. And the 4th one is keeping me awake.

With every new piece, it got exciting.

I used every detail I knew about myself to make the following strategy.

## Cleaning Drafts, aka working on old ideas

I am raiding my old drafts like finding the right jewellery to go with my favorite dress. ==I have 700+ unfinished pieces in my drafts.== Some have over 1000 words, whereas some barely have 100.

I didn’t think about what I wanted to write. I didn’t want to get artsy and take forever to choose the right topic to make a comeback. This time, I was mechanical. I picked the first piece with 400+ words, and luckily, it was about how I freaked out when [AI took over the writing world](https://medium.com/@shreyabadonia/heres-why-nobody-wants-to-write-anymore-f662e1b7b78d).

> I have 700+ unfinished pieces in my drafts.

Pick an old idea from the drafts. Finish it. Edit it. Publish.
Pick. Finish. Edit. Publish. Pick Finish. Edit. Publish.

That’s the formula I kept repeating in my head. No, waiting for the *Prince Charming* idea and die a spinster. This strategy saved me more time than any other hack in this list.

Most people stop at “ *I don’t know what to write* ” or get confused between multiple ideas, especially when they have 700 drafts. To avoid that decision fatigue, I took what was already half-baked in front of me and prepared a whole meal out of it.

It also made me clean my draft list, allowing me to pick the next idea more quickly.

After deleting 30+ drafts, I am still at 716. Screenshot of my Medium Dashboard

## Creating more than I consumed

I didn’t just happen to open my drafts. Before that, I had to ==beg myself to leave the reel addiction behind==.

[My consumption cycle](https://medium.com/p/b0e02c6c8fc4) was on autopilot; I’d wake up, pick up my phone, and automatically reach my Instagram feed. It was addictive. I had to break the cycle. ==I wanted to create more than I consume.==

Challenging myself to do that was the only way to go. I challenged myself not to open Instagram the first thing in the morning.

I still pick up my phone as soon as I wake up (a habit I am still struggling to quit) but now instead of opening IG, I open Medium. I scroll through some stories and browse through a couple. It made me read more, and also got me hooked on my stats. The low numbers make me open my drafts, and I continue from where I left off last night. It’s a vicious cycle.

Switching from reels to writing was uncomfortable, but it felt exemplary after a few sittings. I could feel my focus getting better. My brain was sharper. I was churning more words every second than I was consuming reels. I was happy.

## Using my mood as fuel

Feeling my mood to fuel my writing has worked like magic. I have been using this mind hack for years. You can find more of them [here](https://medium.com/p/1a6835cbbfc2).

When I regretted my decision to move to Jamaica, I wrote this piece to remind myself of [all the good things](https://medium.com/@shreyabadonia/self-learned-skills-that-help-me-live-a-good-life-as-an-expat-e77eea3476f5) that happened.

When I was missing my beach vacation, I wrote this piece on the connection between [creativity and nature](https://medium.com/p/2c3b106646c2) to replay those emotions.

Now, when I am feeling pumped about getting back to writing, I am writing this one to fuel that energy.

It’s so much fun when you expand your emotions into what you’re doing. It’s an effortless way to create and express.

Writing is therapeutic, and what you write greatly impacts how you feel. So why not write to release emotions and also use those to get to a better place

> **“The creative process is a process of surrender, not control.”
> ― Julia Cameron**

## Using the right tools

I loved playing with my new iPad. I’d carry it in my tote bag to use to draw and write while waiting in cages or on a flight. The confusion about choosing from various tools is draining. I don’t need 10 different tools.

Should I write on my iPad or iPhone

All I need is my phone and my laptop.

- I decide what I want to write on my phone.
- I write the rough drafts on my phone.
- I edit on my laptop the next morning.

I sometimes use [Hemingway](https://hemingwayapp.com/) when I am working on a longer piece. But mostly I believe in my judgment to do the needful edits.

==I also used to take help from ChatGPT to edit, but now I am reducing my use of AI even if it’s for editing.==

The fewer tools I have, the more I focus on writing.

## Avoiding decision fatigue

What to write. When to write. Where to write.

The *What, Why, Where* stops more writing than no skillset.

Surprisingly, these decisions are self-inflicted. Deep down, we know the answers, but we still ask them every time we sit down to write. It’s [procrastination in disguise](https://medium.com/p/2b58bfdaba8f).

I [designed](https://medium.com/p/4a84920f3911) my day and space in such a way that I didn’t have to waste my time making decisions.

- Wake up.
- Write the first draft on the phone.
- ==Pick up the laptop to edit yesterday’s final draft.==

Write from bed, from couch, from office desk, or from the cafe. Doesn’t matter. As long as you write, it doesn’t matter if you’re writing from an active volcano or secretly in front of your boss. Crazier the place, the crazier the story.

I decide what I want to write a day prior, so when I wake up, I have a draft waiting for me to be devoured like my running shoes that are next to my bed.

## Making it part of my identity

*“Oh, you’re a writer.”*

I used to feel like an imposter whenever I was asked this question. Was I a writer Maybe. Maybe not.

My reply used to be as lackadaisical as a child who lied about eating their vegetables. I was ashamed to answer that question. I hadn’t written anything for a while to be called a writer.

You’re a writer! — used to give me goosebumps. Then it started freaking me out like the demon under my bed.

I knew if I wanted to write again, I had to work on that identity gap. To become a writer, I needed to write. To feel confident about being a writer, I needed to identify as one.

> When you shift your identity, you get rid of everything that’s stopping you from being that.

Going through my past work, reading the best comments I have received over the years, helped me tap into that identity. Not publishing every day doesn’t make me less of a writer; writing often does. And I never stopped writing for myself during this break.

This paradigm shift helped me get back to writing faster than any of the above points. When you shift your identity, you get rid of everything that’s stopping you from being that.

==Now, being a writer isn’t just something I do, it’s part of who I am.==
