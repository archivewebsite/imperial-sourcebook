# Decision Filter

![[Pasted image 20260503031514.png]]

**Stop trying to make “brilliant” decisions. Start making fewer stupid ones.** Humanity has somehow made this a radical concept, but here we are.

Before any important decision, run it through this five-question filter:

1. **Inversion: “How could this fail”**
    Instead of only asking how to succeed, imagine the plan has failed and work backward. This is similar to a premortem. Research cited by Gary Klein found that “prospective hindsight,” imagining a future failure as already having happened, improved people’s ability to identify reasons for future outcomes by about 30%. ([CLTR](https://cltr.nl/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/Project-Pre-Mortem-HBR-Gary-Klein.pdfutm_source=chatgpt.com "F0709A_pdf.fm - CLTR"))

2. **Second-order thinking: “And then what”**
    A good decision is not just about the first result. It is about the chain reaction. A workout feels bad today but improves health later. Skipping hard work feels good today but quietly invoices you later, with interest, because apparently consequences have accounting departments.

3. **Circle of competence: “Do I actually understand this”**
    Munger’s broader “mental models” approach emphasized using ideas from multiple fields rather than forcing every problem through one narrow lens. The practical lesson is simple: know where you have judgment, know where you are guessing, and do not confuse confidence with competence. ([Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/great-talks/a-lesson-on-worldly-wisdom/utm_source=chatgpt.com "A Lesson on Elementary Worldly Wisdom As It Relates To Investment ..."))

4. **Opportunity cost: “What am I saying no to”**
    Every yes spends time, money, attention, or energy. Research on “opportunity cost neglect” found that people often fail to actively consider the alternatives displaced by a purchase or decision, and reminders about opportunity cost can change choices. ([OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/36/4/553/1787808utm_source=chatgpt.com "Opportunity Cost Neglect | Journal of Consumer Research | Oxford Academic"))

5. **Compounding: “What happens if I repeat this for a year”**
    Small actions matter because repetition turns them into identity, skill, reputation, or regret. The famous “1% better every day” idea is mathematically powerful because compounding turns tiny repeated gains into large long-term differences, though real life is messier than a spreadsheet, annoyingly. ([James Clear](https://jamesclear.com/marginal-gainsutm_source=chatgpt.com "Marginal Gains: This Coach Improved Every Tiny Thing by 1 Percent"))


The practical rule:

**Before committing to anything meaningful, ask: “What would make this fail, what happens next, do I understand it, what am I giving up, and what if I repeat this”**

That single checklist will improve decisions in work, money, habits, relationships, and learning. Not because it makes you a genius, tragically, but because it protects you from the usual human hobby: walking confidently into avoidable problems.
