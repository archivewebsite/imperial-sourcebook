# Four Important Life Skills Psychologists Learn

## Communication skills psychologists have that can inspire you to adapt into your life.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*aNHn_TPeghxvecSK)

Photo by Mathieu Stern on Unsplash

In our daily lives, we liken a bunch of things to therapy. We say, “meeting up with my friend felt like therapy”, or going to that pottery class felt like therapy. Watching that comedy show or going to a musical felt like therapy.

But in truth, therapy is not something that makes you feel good all the time; in fact, it’s the place where you show your most vulnerable self. You cry during the sessions, and you encounter thoughts that you sometimes feel ashamed to express loudly. You become unarmed.

Therapy is not like those activities that give you short-term bursts of joy; it makes you think later on, and it prepares and equips you with the right tools and mindset for long-lasting change.

But through that exposure to your most vulnerable sides, with the help of the right guidance and the safe space your therapist provides, you’re able to rise from there and see significant improvement.

How do psychotherapists enable this change? Psychotherapists don’t own a magical wand or have special powers that enable them to change their clients’ lives from scratch.

What they obtain instead is some characteristics, techniques, and years of continuous training to make them better at helping other humans, understanding their needs, and offering solutions.

We all have these skills as humans, to varying degrees. Listening, showing empathy, showing compassion… What makes psychotherapists different is that they work on these skills even more and add more to them.

It’s also not a coincidence that people with such characteristics aim towards careers in this field, so they already have a base for it, but they build on top of it during years of training and reading.

But even psychotherapists are not fully sufficient in those skills; it’s always an ongoing journey. To empathize, to actively listen, to detect important clues and identify maladaptive thoughts in someone’s speech, gestures, and emotions is something therapists can only get better at, but not reach perfection.

But they are indeed better at this compared to the average human because of the requirements of their career title, and their willingness, curiosity, and inclination to understand and help other people to reach better versions of themselves, which drove them to pursue this career in the first place.

These qualities they hold also exist in other people in varying degrees that are not psychologists per se, but psychologists are trained to bring together and harmonize all these skills, and they do it with the awareness that they are doing it and by knowing how it will potentially benefit the person sitting across them in the therapy room.

Of course, this is not to say that you need to act like a psychologist when interacting with someone; that would feel weird and not like a reciprocal, healthy relationship.

Even a psychologist should draw borders between their work and their actual interactions with friends and people in their lives that are not their clients.

But it’s hard not to think about what would happen if people were better at listening to the other person, empathizing with them, and not rushing to give advice; maybe the world would become somewhat better and improve in some ways?

So here are some of the qualities of psychologists that everyone can strive to become better at, take inspiration from, and adapt to their lives, relationships, and repertoire of communication styles.

## They are active, careful listeners

[Listening is the most important skill](https://medium.com/writing-cooperative/how-psychology-can-inspire-you-to-become-a-better-writer-cb02ca2fc13b) in a psychologist’s toolbox, much more important than all the techniques they can learn. Because through listening, they can understand the person sitting across from them, their motives, and thus don’t repeat stereotypical advice and solutions.

Through listening and understanding, they can empathize with the other person. Listening is the core skill they need to work on and master.

Psychotherapists have the ability to listen to you attentively, identify recurring themes in your different life problems that you bring to therapy, and show that they understand you.

Through doing that, they are able to challenge your thoughts and core beliefs, offer you new perspectives and ideas through scientifically proven methods, in a way that serves your needs.

Listening sounds easy, but it’s a skill you can always get better at, and most people fail at it. We drift off in conversations, or we interrupt the other person speaking. We grow impatient, and we fail to see the meaning behind some words and unspoken words.

Active listening is something you can improve during your lifetime, and it’s actually not as easy as it sounds. Only through effective listening can you ask useful questions and make useful interpretations, and only through listening can you pinpoint the root causes of the problems.

Listening is also not sitting in silence and letting the other person talk all the time. You might look like you’re effectively listening when you do that, you can at least fool them for the time being that you’re listening.

But what matters is that you give your attention to what has been said, and also studying the body language of the client, the language that the client uses to express their situation, and their recurring thought patterns.

## They are nonjudgmental

One of the distinguishing factors that separates psychologists from others is that they are much less judgmental than the regular folk.

In our daily lives, we judge others, we blame, and we assume things about others, all the time. Our brain makes all sorts of assumptions.

We’re quick to put a label on people, group them, stigmatize, blame, or criticize them for what they did, how they look, sound, and behave, before understanding the context of the situation and that person’s perspective.

We do this because it’s automatic and it’s easy. But by doing that, we fail to understand, we fail to hear the meaning behind their words and actions. After all, every person has a motive behind an action. It doesn’t have to make them right, but it helps in heaps to know the story behind it.

How do psychologists do this? Psychologists don’t assume; they ask. They ask to understand that person’s unique perspective on the situation.

They are aware that every person’s situation can be evaluated differently, depending on the context and the background of the person, the beliefs and thought patterns they hold.

They don’t make comments like, “why would you do something like that”, “if you did this, it means you’re…” etc., or put a label on someone.

Being nonjudgmental is not the easiest, and can be harder in our personal relationships compared to clients, but it’s a skill that can be improved in heaps, and something we can learn a lot from psychotherapists, which can lead to amazing improvements in our relationships.

Instead of lashing out at a friend immediately, you can try to understand them from their perspective, empathize with them, and think about why they might have done this and why it might be triggering you that they did something like this. Maybe it triggers a deeper wound within you that your friend has no idea about triggering.

## They try to understand & and don’t rush to give advice

Closely tied with not being judgmental, psychotherapists try to understand the situation at hand and the motives behind their clients’ actions before making any assumptions or comments and jumping to conclusions.

Even if it means four entire sessions of evaluation before doing any type of intervention, they don’t hastily try to offer a solution and give surface-level advice, but first try to understand the situation and the person sitting across them.

They show that they understand through listening, by paraphrasing their words, reflecting on what they said, and asking relevant questions, questions that could help the client to discover a deeper thought or connection.

Usually, in our friendships and communications in real life, when our friends tell us something, we’re quick to jump to conclusions and offer advice. While the intention is usually good, it’s usually not helpful.

Most people want to be heard and listened to and find someone who understands them and where they’re coming from.

When someone is pouring their heart out to you, you can get the urge to say something like “why didn’t you try this? Or this will fix your situation,” but not acting on that urge and just being present with them, sharing their perspective, is what is invaluable and creates that healing effect.

We get that urge because we want to be helpful, and sometimes we even want to be useful. But when someone is emotional and talking about an issue that has been bothering them for a long time, it can be disheartening when you give generic, repeated, or even helpful advice that doesn’t feel related to their personal experience at all.

They probably thought about it anyway, and what they need is to be heard, not be corrected. By doing that, you’re almost shoving their words back into their mouths and disvalidating their feelings.

I’m not saying not to offer any solutions; problem-solving from a different perspective is valuable too, and sometimes good, appropriate advice can be really effective.

What I’m saying is to first listen and be there for them and understand them before offering reheated advice. And when you give advice, consider them and their feelings, and be careful about not talking in definite terms.

Yes, their thoughts can be irrational too, and you might get the urge to fix them, but usually, what looks nonchallenging to you can be really challenging for someone who’s inside that situation and dealing with its repercussions with their own set of coping skills.

Sometimes, before trying to change something, you need to be aware of your feelings and accept them, and not blame yourself for having them.

## They give the spotlight to you

Therapy is not like friendship or any other relationship. In therapy, the focus is fully on the client and what they bring to therapy. The spotlight is on them. The therapist doesn’t share their personal lives or turn therapy into a friendly conversation.

Therapists can share a personal experience only if they think it will help their client’s situation, and it’s important to use this sparingly, too, not all the time. Because it’s not about you, it’s about them.

Of course, other relationships are not like this, but I think other relationships can take something from this. Usually, in conversations, we try to dominate the dialogue, and can talk too much without giving the other people any space, and some people don’t even realize that they’re doing this.

This can be a hard balance to catch for most, but it will be helpful to look back and think every once in a while during the conversation whether you gave the other person space to express themselves, listened to them, or interrupted them.

When written like this, it seems obvious, but you’d be surprised how many people do this. Giving the spotlight to others when needed, sharing their emotions, their grief, sadness, or success and joy, even makes you more liked and respected by the person, and improves the personal relationship.

## Last words

Training to become a psychotherapist and gaining experience in the therapy room in the therapist’s chair made me learn and improve a lot of these skills that I’ve talked about, and enabled me to transfer those skills I learned to my daily life.

Because all of us can benefit from being less judgmental and better listeners, and we can work on these skills to have more effective communication and understanding of others, the human condition, show more empathy and flexibility in our thoughts about others and ourselves.

It’s an ongoing process to hone these skills, which comes with embracing the notion that you’ll always be on a learning path, but that’s part of life anyway.
