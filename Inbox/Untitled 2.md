# Trump and Xi’s Beijing Summit: Ceremony, Ambiguity, and the Search for Stability

## A Summit Built on Spectacle

The meeting between President Donald Trump and President Xi Jinping in Beijing was designed to look historic. From the beginning, China staged the visit with imperial-level symbolism: a welcome ceremony on Tiananmen Square, goose-stepping soldiers, cheering children, a private tour of the Temple of Heaven, talks over lunch and tea, and a walk through the gardens of Zhongnanhai, the secretive leadership compound at the heart of Chinese power.

Trump had reportedly wanted China to surpass the grandeur of his 2017 Beijing visit. On that point, Xi seemed willing to oblige. The symbolism mattered because this was not merely a diplomatic meeting; it was a carefully choreographed display of status, hierarchy, and personal rapport.

Trump appeared pleased with the treatment. He described his relationship with Xi in warm terms and said the relationship between China and the United States would become “better than ever before.” Xi echoed the friendly language, calling for the two countries to work together on global challenges and bring greater stability to the world.

Yet beneath the ceremony, the summit produced more confusion than clarity. Both sides claimed to want stability, but they offered noticeably different accounts of what had actually been agreed. The result was a summit of competing narratives: one in which Washington emphasized business deals, trade mechanisms, and geopolitical commitments, while Beijing highlighted Taiwan, strategic stability, and the long-term structure of the US-China relationship.

## The Politics of Flattery

One of the striking themes of the visit was the degree to which China appeared to tailor the summit to Trump personally. The tour of the Temple of Heaven was especially symbolic. It is a 15th-century imperial complex where Chinese emperors once prayed for good harvests. For Trump, the visit offered the sense of a rare honor. For Xi, it allowed him to present China as an ancient civilization with enduring grandeur.

The optics also carried a deeper political message. In China, Xi has increasingly cultivated an imperial image of leadership. In the United States, Trump’s presidency has often blurred the line between democratic office and monarchical performance. The summit brought these two styles together: two powerful leaders, both fond of personal authority, meeting amid symbols of state power and historical greatness.

Even the lighter moments were revealing. During the state banquet, China’s military band reportedly played “YMCA,” one of Trump’s favorite songs. On one level, this was simply theatrical hospitality. On another, it showed how carefully Beijing had studied Trump’s preferences and how willing it was to use ceremony as a diplomatic tool.

For Xi, the pageantry served several purposes. It projected confidence to a domestic audience. It showed China as a nation capable of hosting the American president on equal or superior terms. It also helped stabilize a relationship that had been badly shaken by trade conflict, geopolitical tension, and recent crises involving Venezuela and the Middle East.

## A Personal Relationship, but Not the Whole Story

The summit repeatedly emphasized the personal bond between Trump and Xi. Wu Xinbo, a professor and dean at Fudan University’s Institute of International Studies, described the relationship between the two leaders as unusually personal compared with previous US-China presidential relationships.

But the transcript also makes clear that Beijing does not view personal warmth as the main objective. From the Chinese perspective, the personal relationship is useful only insofar as it helps manage the state-to-state relationship. Trump may like to speak in terms of friendship, but Xi’s priorities appear more strategic: reduce uncertainty, avoid uncontrolled escalation, and secure a framework that gives China room to maneuver.

That distinction is important. The ceremony may have been designed partly for Trump’s benefit, but China’s goals were not merely theatrical. Beijing wanted a more predictable relationship with Washington, especially after a period of intense economic and military uncertainty.

## Trade: A Framework Without the Details

Trade was expected to be one of the central issues of the summit. Before the meeting, both sides had signaled interest in extending a trade truce reached during an earlier meeting in South Korea. Preparatory talks had reportedly made progress. Yet after the summit, there was no clear announcement extending the truce in concrete terms.

Instead, the two sides appeared to agree on creating a Board of Trade and a Board of Investment. These bodies would theoretically allow officials from both countries to address disputes more quickly than existing mechanisms, many of which have been ignored or bypassed.

Wu Xinbo suggested that the lack of specific announcements did not mean the summit failed. In his view, both sides may have agreed on a broader framework while leaving technical details to working-level officials. Rather than negotiating issue by issue, tariffs one day and export controls the next, the summit may have aimed to define the overall parameters of economic relations.

That approach fits China’s long-standing preference for broad principles before details. It also fits Trump’s own style of dealmaking: announce a big framework, then let others fill in the fine print.

Still, the uncertainty matters. American officials pointed to several possible deals, including Chinese purchases of Boeing aircraft and agricultural products such as soybeans. But China had not confirmed these as finalized contracts. Wu argued that Beijing likely sees them as general understandings rather than signed business deals. The United States, by contrast, had political incentives to present them as major wins.

This difference captures a broader problem in the summit: the two sides may have agreed on language, but not necessarily on meaning.

## Business Deals and Political Messaging

For Trump, the economic deliverables mattered partly because they fit his political brand. He wants to present himself as a leader who extracts major concessions from foreign powers. Boeing aircraft, soybeans, and agricultural purchases offer tangible talking points.

But the political impact at home may be limited. John Prideaux, The Economist’s US editor, argued that even large purchases of American goods are unlikely to transform US domestic politics. Voters remain more concerned about prices, especially fuel prices, and the economic effects of wider geopolitical instability.

The soybean issue also carries historical baggage. China made similar purchase commitments during the earlier phase-one trade deal, but those purchases did not fully materialize at the expected scale. That history makes some American observers skeptical of new promises.

The result is a familiar pattern: Trump can claim a deal, China can avoid confirming too much, and the actual economic consequences remain uncertain.

## Iran: One Meeting, Two Readouts

The summit also exposed major differences over Iran and the Middle East. According to the American readout, the two leaders agreed that Iran should not have a nuclear weapon, that the Strait of Hormuz should be reopened, and that no country or organization should be allowed to charge tolls there. Trump went further, claiming that Xi had pledged not to provide weapons to Iran while it was at war with America.

China’s account was far more restrained. Beijing emphasized dialogue, peace, stability, and a ceasefire-based reopening of the Strait of Hormuz. It did not confirm Trump’s claim about weapons.

This gap is significant. China may oppose nuclear proliferation in principle, but it is highly unlikely to publicly endorse American or Israeli military action to prevent Iran from acquiring nuclear weapons. Beijing generally resists the idea that one state can use coercive force to dictate another state’s behavior.

The Iran discussion therefore illustrates the broader pattern of the summit: Washington appeared to portray Chinese restraint as agreement, while Beijing used vaguer language that preserved its diplomatic flexibility.

## Taiwan: The Most Dangerous Issue

Of all the topics discussed, Taiwan may have been the most consequential. Beijing wanted Taiwan near the top of the agenda, especially after Trump approved a large arms package for Taiwan earlier in the year. China saw that move as deeply provocative and wanted the summit to produce some form of American restraint.

Xi reportedly warned Trump that mishandling Taiwan could lead to conflict between China and the United States. That warning was not subtle. It was a direct reminder that Taiwan remains the most dangerous flashpoint in the relationship.

Trump, during the formal summit, said little publicly about the issue. But after leaving Beijing, he made comments that caused concern in Taiwan. He reportedly said he had discussed arms sales to Taiwan with Xi in detail and described those arms sales as a bargaining chip. That is sensitive because the United States has long maintained commitments to Taiwan that discourage treating its security as something to be negotiated with Beijing.

Trump also portrayed Taiwan’s president, Lai Ching-te, as someone who could drag the United States into war by pushing toward independence. That framing closely resembles Beijing’s own argument: that Taiwan’s leadership, not China’s pressure, is the source of instability.

For China, those remarks would have been welcome. For Taiwan, they were alarming.

## Arms Sales and the Question of Trust

The key practical question is whether Trump will approve another arms package for Taiwan before Xi’s expected visit to the United States in September. Wu Xinbo suggested that if Trump did approve such a package before the visit, Xi might cancel the trip. From Beijing’s perspective, that would be a public slap in the face after Xi had already delivered his warning.

John Prideaux offered a more cautious interpretation. Trump’s words on Taiwan may sound soft toward Beijing, but his actions have sometimes been tougher. He has approved significant arms sales to Taiwan, and Congress remains strongly hawkish on China. That means any indefinite delay in arms sales would likely face political resistance in Washington.

Still, Trump’s rhetoric has consequences. Even if US policy does not formally change, uncertainty about Trump’s willingness to defend Taiwan can shape Taiwanese politics. If voters in Taiwan begin to believe that the United States cannot be trusted, parties favoring closer ties with mainland China may gain strength. That would serve Xi’s long-term objective without requiring immediate military action.

## “Constructive Strategic Stability”

Another major outcome of the summit was the phrase “constructive strategic stability.” This appears to be a new formula for defining US-China relations.

Wu Xinbo explained the phrase through its three key words. “Stability” means preventing the relationship from sliding back into crisis. “Strategic” means extending that stability beyond short-term trade freezes and into broader questions of security, diplomacy, and global affairs. “Constructive” means avoiding a Cold War-style stability based only on deterrence and fear, and instead seeking cooperation where possible while managing competition.

In theory, the phrase sounds positive. In practice, it is vague. Prideaux suggested that many in Washington may not know exactly what it means. But for Beijing, such language matters a great deal. China has long sought formulas that put the United States and China on more equal footing. Getting Washington to accept a phrase like this allows Beijing to claim that the relationship is being redefined on terms closer to China’s preference.

It also gives China a rhetorical tool. If the United States later takes actions Beijing dislikes, especially on Taiwan, China can argue that Washington is violating the spirit of “constructive strategic stability.”

## China’s View of the Balance of Power

The summit also revealed how Xi sees the broader global order. He appears to view China as rising and the United States as declining in relative terms. His references to major historical transformation and the possibility of great-power conflict suggest that Beijing sees the world entering a new era, one in which American dominance is weakening.

Trump, according to the transcript, seemed to interpret Xi’s comments about American decline as a criticism of the Biden era rather than of the United States more broadly. But Xi’s meaning was likely wider. From Beijing’s perspective, the US-led global order is under strain, and China has an opportunity to shape what comes next.

This is one reason China wants stability with the United States. Beijing does not necessarily want open confrontation now. It wants time, predictability, and space to strengthen its position. A chaotic American administration creates risks, but it may also create opportunities if Trump is willing to prioritize personal diplomacy and transactional deals over long-term alliance commitments.

## A Truce, Not a Settlement

The summit did not resolve the deepest problems in US-China relations. It did not clearly extend the trade truce. It did not settle disputes over Taiwan. It did not produce a shared position on Iran. It did not eliminate competition over technology, artificial intelligence, security, or global influence.

What it did produce was a temporary lowering of temperature. Both sides seemed to agree that uncontrolled escalation would be dangerous. Both sides also agreed to keep talking, with Xi expected to visit the United States later in the year.

That is not nothing. US-China relations have been through periods of severe tension, and a mechanism for managing disputes is better than no mechanism at all. But the summit’s stability remains fragile because it rests on ambiguous language, competing interpretations, and the personal instincts of two leaders with very different political systems and strategic goals.

The Beijing summit was therefore less a breakthrough than a pause. It offered ceremony without certainty, warmth without trust, and a framework without details. For now, that may be enough to prevent a crisis. But it is not enough to resolve the rivalry.
