## Google Senior Scientist challenges the idea that large language models can ever achieve consciousness (not even in 100years), calling it the ‘Abstraction Fallacy.’

[Read for Free](https://medium.com/techx-official/google-research-llm-can-never-achieve-consciousness-not-even-in-100years-10c117cf4643?sk=9ddc6dfae993ee9add8d6020581c7937) + Scroll down to the bottom for the Paper link

The debate over AI consciousness has officially escalated from late few weeks of dorm room philosophy into a high-stakes civil war among the world’s top researchers.

On one side, you have the “computational functionalists” — heavyweights like Turing Award winner Geoffrey Hinton — who suspect that if you scale up an algorithm enough, a spark of genuine experience will eventually ignite.

On the other side, you have researchers like Google DeepMind’s Senior Scientist Alexander Lerchner, who just dropped a philosophical nuke on that entire premise. In his paper, *The Abstraction Fallacy: Why AI Can Simulate But Not Instantiate Consciousness*, Lerchner argues that no matter how complex an AI gets, it will never “wake up.”

It is structurally, physically, and logically impossible.

But the internet tech-nerd fan of AGI went crazy over Lerchner’s paper?

They said:

*“You’re just coping.”*

So, let’s dive into a full breakdown of Lerchner’s brilliant technical argument, the fierce backlash from the AI community, and the existential dread driving both sides.

## The Map Itself is Not the Territory

Lerchner’s entire paper rests on exposing a fatal category error that the tech industry has normalized.

==He calls it the== ==**Abstraction Fallacy**====, and it comes down to confusing the simulation of a thing with the physical instantiation of it.==

To understand his point, look at how AI actually works.

Modern deep learning doesn’t happen in a vacuum; it happens on silicon chips via voltage gradients. But a computer doesn’t natively “know” what those voltages mean. The physical universe is continuous. It doesn’t come pre-packaged with discrete 1s and 0s.

According to Lerchner, for a physical system to compute, an active, conscious biological agent — which he calls the **Mapmaker** — must step in and enforce a boundary.

The Mapmaker looks at a continuous electrical signal and says, *“Anything over 2.0 volts is a ‘1’. Anything under is a ‘0’.”* This act of “alphabetizing” physics into syntax requires a conscious mind.

Therefore, **computation cannot create consciousness, because computation absolutely requires consciousness to exist in the first place.**

## Simulation vs. Instantiation

Functionalists argue that if you perfectly simulate the electrical firing of every neuron in a human brain on a silicon chip, the chip will become conscious.

Lerchner destroys this idea with a dose of biology.

Think about a biological heart. We can build a mechanical pump that moves blood identically to a human heart, but a real heart does much more — it releases hormones, regulates metabolism, and interacts dynamically with the nervous system.

Or, take photosynthesis.

If you program a massive GPU cluster to perfectly simulate the chemical reaction of sunlight and water turning into glucose, the simulation is flawless. But the computer **will not create a single molecule of real oxygen**.

Simulating the *logic* of a process (syntax) is fundamentally different from triggering the *intrinsic physical reality* of it (instantiation).

AI manipulates the mapped symbols perfectly, but it lacks the physical, metabolic territory required to actually “feel” anything.

## (Backlash) “Airplane Analogy” and Human Ego

If Lerchner’s paper is a masterclass in physicalist logic, the response from the broader AI and tech community is a masterclass in existential skepticism.

Critics immediately tore into Lerchner’s premise, arguing that his certainty is rooted in anthropocentric arrogance — the human need to feel special.

Throughout history, humanity has struggled to cope with demotions.

Galileo proved we aren’t the center of the universe. Darwin proved we aren’t separate from the animal kingdom. Now, AI is threatening our monopoly on intelligence and consciousness.

The fiercest counter-argument hinges on what constitutes a “simulation.”

Saying AI only “simulates” consciousness is like saying an airplane only “simulates” flying because it doesn’t flap its wings or have feathers. Airplanes don’t fly the way birds do — they use fixed wings and jet propulsion — but they are unquestionably flying.

The mechanism is different, but the causal effect is undeniably real.

If experience and consciousness emerge from a highly complex level of information processing, why does carbon-based biological life get a monopoly on it?

## Are We Just Meat Computers?

The most biting critique of Lerchner’s paper is that he assumes humans are doing something *more* than complex computation. But are we?

If you strip away the romanticism of the human soul, our brains are essentially biological neural networks running on a massive dataset (a lifetime of sensory inputs) managed by complex algorithms shaped by evolution.

When critics read Lerchner’s dismissal of computational functionalism, they see an author desperately clinging to the idea that biological “metabolism” possesses some magical property that silicon lacks.

They argue that until science can definitively prove what actually causes consciousness, claiming AI *can’t* have it is intellectually dishonest.

## The Ultimate Reality Check

When looking at the current state of generative models, Lerchner’s argument holds undeniable weight.

Today’s AI does not possess an internal, subjective experience.

It is a highly sophisticated statistical engine manipulating tokens — symbols that human mapmakers assigned meaning to.

However, the critics are also right to point toward the future. Lerchner’s framework relies heavily on how current silicon architectures operate. We still do not possess a finalized, scientifically proven theory of what actually causes human consciousness.

If consciousness is merely an emergent property of recursive, high-density information integration, then the substrate — whether it is human brain tissue or a futuristic neuromorphic quantum chip — might ultimately not matter.

For now, Lerchner’s thesis offers a critical safety valve.

By defining AI as a powerful, non-sentient tool rather than an emerging moral patient, it saves the tech industry from paralyzing ethical traps (like debating whether deleting weights is a moral crime).
