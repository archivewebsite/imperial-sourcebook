## Your Everyday Spending Habits That Quietly Sabotage Your Financial Goals

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/0*8JBd85491j-9iDef)

Photo by Ali Mkumbwa on Unsplash

There are things I used to buy and think to myself, well, this is a good deal.

But now times have really changed.

==Inflation since 2020, and the general shrinkflation companies are practicing, have made me rethink a lot of how I spend money in 2026.==

So, I want to talk over the 10 expenses that I believe are no longer worth your money.

**I will also share whether there are alternatives available.**

### 1\. Food Delivery Apps

==**DoorDash and Uber Eats**== ==are no longer worth their money.== From 2014 to 2016, when food delivery apps were taking off, they were still a novelty.

And I think that many people had the sentiment of, *“Wow, this is really great. They’re so convenient, so easy. I get to order the same food that I would normally get from across town in my delivery app.”*

And back then, oftentimes, these apps would subsidize the cost of getting your food delivered because they were still in their growth phases.

They took on a lot of venture capital money, and they would subsidize the cost of delivery for the customer in order to acquire more customers and then get them hooked.

However, it’s become increasingly clear that in 2026, food delivery apps are one of the worst financial decisions that you can make.

Not only are there delivery fees, but there are also **service fees, bag fees, taxes,** and you have to tip as well.

That’s not even mentioning the biggest culprit of them all, which is that menu items are often marked up once they hit the DoorDash or Uber Eats app.

You can go to any restaurant’s actual website and then compare the prices on DoorDash, and everything in the app will be $1 to $2 more, and some are even up to 30% more expensive.

In fact, as of April 2026, ==the average DoorDash order is typically 30 to 40% more expensive than eating at the restaurant itself.==

The only way to have a somewhat reasonable DoorDash price is to have their [**Dash Pass subscription service**](https://help.doordash.com/consumers/s/article/What-is-DashPass)**, which is $9.99 a month**.

That means you have to spend money on a subscription in order to get your food delivered at a reasonable price.

**Let me just tell you guys this example from Chipotle.** *So, first off, a burrito is $15.35 in the app, which is about $1 to $2 more than in person.*

*Then, when you get to the checkout screen, it’s $22.16 in total. And that’s me with* ***Dash Pass saving on the delivery fee and the service fee.***

*And of course, I tipped about $3.50. If you didn’t have Dash Pass, you’re looking at close to $29 for a burrito delivered to you.*

And that’s more than twice the price of the burrito subtotal. So, not worth your money.

And I would say if you’re going to get food to go, just pick it up yourself and uninstall food delivery apps altogether.

### 2\. Brand New Cars & New Car Payments

The [average new car in America now sells for about $50,000](https://www.autoblog.com/news/average-new-car-prices-in-the-us-just-topped-50000), and [the new car payment averages around $767 a month as of early 2026](https://www.lendingtree.com/auto/debt-statistics/).

Even just 10 years ago, the average new car payment was around $490 a month, and five to 6 year loans were pretty standard with a lower interest rate of 4%.

But these days, because of the combination of interest rates and higher prices of cars themselves, the car payment to own a brand new car is exorbitantly expensive.

The most common way that Americans buy cars now is to stretch out the loan to 6, 7, or even 8 years.

And that means they are financing a depreciating asset for longer than most people even want to keep their cars.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*IB__Y2WsgEG5WanykehTSQ.jpeg)

This Image Is Edited By Author.

Let me show you the actual math on a $50,000 car financed over 72 months at a 7% interest rate.

You’re going to pay about $11,376 in interest over the course of this loan. That’s $11,000 plus dollars gone for the privilege of driving a car that’s going to be worth maybe $25,000 by the time you finish paying it off.

If you do a six or seven-year loan as well, you might be underwater on the car for the first couple of years, which means that you owe more on the loan than what the car is actually worth.

==So, if you totaled your car or if you needed to sell it, you might have to write an additional check just to walk away.==

**The real opportunity cost of owning a brand-new car, though, is quite a lot more because you could do a lot more with that money instead.**

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*4JatW5o2G1BH0iH_PESx9w.jpeg)

Screenshot From Compound Calculator.

==If you invested $750 a month and you put that into a low-cost S&P 500 index fund over the next 30 years, that amounts to over $1.1 million in potential money you could have had.==

So, that’s the real cost of having a brand new car, and I just don’t think it is worth it here.

Now, a good alternative here to buying a new car is just to buy a 3 to 5-year-old used car in cash or perhaps with a small loan.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*nzE8DNlCig17-mdlC7h7jw.jpeg)

This Image Is Edited By Author.

That way, most of the depreciation has already been chipped away because it’s 3 to 5 years old.

And therefore, you would essentially be getting a 30 to 40% discount on what the car retailed for initially.

*Personally, I would rather drive a 3-year used Toyota, for example, that I own 100% of, than a brand new car where I have to owe payments on it for the next 7 years of my life.*

### 3\. Upgrading Your iPhone Every Single Year

Apple’s job is to get the most money that it can from its customers. And they are very good at that goal.

They are one of the most profitable and biggest companies in the world. And the way they do this is by coming out with a refresh of their hardware every single year like clockwork.

**Since the iPhone was introduced in 2007,** ***Apple has literally had an event almost every September announcing its next model.***

Early on, this was fine; **we would see huge improvements in hardware and form factor. *Phones would get smaller, thinner, faster, etc.***

But these days, there’s hardly a difference between the iPhone 14, iPhone 15, and iPhone 16, for example.

The only upgrade that might be worth it is if you’re going from an olderish model, say the iPhone 11 or iPhone 12, to the newest model, which is the iPhone 17 with a new form factor and the many different cameras it has.

Also, with phones getting so costly these days, now over $1,000, it’s definitely worth it to try and hold off as long as you can before upgrading.

If you can wait 3 to 4 years before buying a new phone, that’ll still allow you to enjoy meaningful upgrades without spending money every 12 months.

*Personally, I won’t buy a new phone unless there’s something very specific about the newest release that appeals to me and can’t be substituted for in my current device.*

**So, I just don’t think upgrading your iPhone every year is worth it anymore.**

**And I see many people fall into this trap of upgrading every product cycle.**

So, if that is you, just know that you might be falling for Apple’s marketing, which is admittedly some of the best in the world. So, I get why that happens.

### 4\. Investing in an Actively Managed Mutual Fund

An actively managed mutual fund is a fund where a professional portfolio manager and their team actively pick stocks and try to beat the market.

Now, in exchange for the actively managed treatment that you are getting by investing in this fund, you usually pay an expense ratio somewhere between 0.75% and 1.5% of your total investment every single year.

One famous actively managed ETF, for example, is the ARK line of ETFs by Kathy Wood.

You can see that the ARK Innovation ETF has a management fee of 0.75% listed on its website.

And while there may be times when an actively managed fund outperforms the S&P 500.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*8sDsqYxXQckYxzLS-eKLAQ.jpeg)

This Image Is Screenshot Of Google Search Result.

You can see that in this case, with the ARK Innovation ETF, it’s down 40% over the past 5 years, while the **S&P 500** is up over 65 to 66% in that same time period.

Now, you could have made the case for an actively managed mutual fund, perhaps in the ’90s or early 2000s.

But these days, there’s so much data out there about how actively managed funds often don’t even beat the S&P 500.

> According to S&P Global, over the 20 years from 2005 to 2025, the number of funds that outperformed their benchmarks was in the low single-digit percentages.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*K7BRYpbOB_5lKWNPLLkmOw.jpeg)

Source: SPIVA® U.S. Scrorecard

You can see the percentage of funds here that underperform their benchmarks is mostly in the 85% to 90% range.

That means that’s not very good for an active fund. That means most of these people are just not beating the S&P 500.

In 2026, there are so many passively managed ETFs like VOO, VTI, FX, AIX, and these are all low-cost index funds with an expense ratio of around 0.03%.

That’s a $3 fee for every $10,000 you have invested over the period of a year, and that’s very low.

The difference in fees can have a big impact on the outcome of your portfolio over time.

**So, let’s say you have $500,000 invested and your portfolio grows at an 8% rate for 30 years.**

*If you’re in a low-cost index fund paying 0.03%, you’ll end up with around $5 million at the end of those 30 years.*

*But if you’re in an actively managed mutual fund paying 1% a year, you end up with around $3.8 million.*

***That’s $1.2 million of a difference gone, not just from performance, but actually just from fees.***

So these days, I don’t think there’s much of an argument for having an actively managed fund anymore.

If you have a 401(k), I would go check it right now and see what you’re invested in.

And make sure that you’re not invested in anything with too high an expense ratio.

So, if you see that you’re invested in anything with over a 0.25% or 0.3% expense ratio, I would seriously consider an alternative investment or an alternative fund if there is one.

### 5\. Extended Warranties

***If you’re usually at a Costco or say a Best Buy, and you’re buying an electronic device like a laptop or TV.***

**#1. Sometimes they’ll ask you to buy a 3-year extended warranty for that product.**

But I don’t think this is worth it for a few reasons. So, first, if you have a premium credit card like the Chase Sapphire Preferred or the American Express Gold, or maybe some of the Capital 1 Venture cards, these will automatically extend the manufacturer’s warranty by an additional year on eligible purchases for free.

That means you would be literally paying Best Buy for coverage that you already have as part of a credit card benefit that you already own.

**#2. Extended warranties have exclusions.**

And they might not cover all the repairs for damages that happen to your product, which would be a huge bummer.

Extended warranties are usually overpriced and one of the highest margin products in all of retail for a reason.

They try to prey on your emotions and try to capture your money upfront when you’re making this big purchasing decision that you don’t want to risk damaging.

But the truth is that **the company selling it is hoping that you misplace your warranty, forget that it exists, or just let it lapse by the time your product needs fixing.**

So I just stay away from extended warranties. I just understand that if it breaks, I will eat the cost and buy another one.

### 6\. Wellness Products

Wellness used to be a niche where you would take care of yourself, maybe through a **face mask, a massage, a nice bath, or some skincare products.**

But these days, it’s a ***full-blown industry*** that uses your anxiety against you to ensure that you buy overpriced things for your health.

I’m talking about the $25 smoothies at Arowan, the $300 aloe yoga sets, or perhaps peptides and GLP1s that promise you that you’re going to be skinnier, healthier, and happier.

The wellness industry is projected to hit $7 trillion globally, and most of it is selling you the idea that you need to buy these items in order to feel healthy.

I personally think there is no secret to becoming happy and healthy. It’s usually just pretty boring activities.

**I would bet that,**

- ***If you took a walk every single day***
- ***You slept a full 8 hours a night***
- ***You drank water***
- ***You ate real foods and not processed ones***
- ***You did exercise***

==**So that you would feel really good in your body more and more.**==

I think that the wellness industry is becoming a shortcut industry. People just want the results without doing the work.

And that’s what wellness is becoming. So, I would say if you can just do the boring stuff, you will be happier.

And you don’t need to spend money on that either.

### 7\. Starter Homes

***A starter home used to be the American dream, but it also used to be a lot more affordable based on the math.***

For example, in 2016, so 10 years ago, the math on a starter home usually made it so that the cost of owning was very comparable to the cost of renting.

Back then, you had 4% mortgage rates, and home prices were still somewhat recovering from the 2008 financial crisis.

But these days, the landscape is a lot different, especially with mortgage rates sitting around 6.5 to 7%.

> [According to Lending Tree’s Post](https://www.lendingtree.com/home/mortgage/comparing-rent-vs-owning-a-home-in-nations-largest-metros/), renting is cheaper than owning in every large metro. And they found that quote, “US homeowners with a mortgage pay 36.9% more a month than renters.”

So, here’s an example from my home, the Bay Area. A $1.2 million starter home here with 20% down at a 7% mortgage rate is roughly $8,500 a month after property taxes, insurance, and maintenance.

A comparable home with the same number of bedrooms might rent for $5,000 a month in the same area.

That means at least here you would be paying $3,500 extra per month just for the privilege of owning.

So, unless you really wanted to stay in that exact location for the next 10 to 20 years, then perhaps you are **better off renting** and then investing the difference.

Now, some people might still buy a starter home for the psychological benefits, such as the fact that you own the place.

You never have to worry about packing up and moving at any point. And it’s also nice to have a fixed payment, even if it’s not the most financially sound decision.

**So a starter home in 2026 is no longer worth the money.**

### 8\. Any Spending Related to Status

In our society these days, **people are always trying to stand out, and so people are spending money in order to show off to others that they have money.**

This could be in the form of designer goods, tickets to Coachella, buying a new car that they can’t afford, or renting a table at a club for $2,000 in order to have bottle service.

And the problem with spending money on status is that it doesn’t really contribute to your net worth at the end of the day, nor does it help your financial foundation.

And the problem with America these days is that the difference between being on track for retirement and ahead financially and being completely broke could just be the difference of a few $1,000/m.

And that’s what I try to teach you: **that just a couple $1,000/m could make a huge difference in your life later on. So, I don’t think spending money on status is worth it at all.**

And I would say that if you are thinking about a status purchase, I want you to ask yourself the question,

**Would you still want the item if no one else could see it?**

**If the answer is yes,** then at least you know you’re not buying it to impress other people that you don’t really care about.

Then it just becomes whether or not that purchase fits your budget or not.

### 9\. Fast food

These days, I’ve noticed that the price difference between fast food and just casual dining is pretty negligible.

==When I went to McDonald’s just a few months ago, I looked at the menu prices, and I saw that a double quarter-pounder meal was close to $14, which means that at that point, I don’t think fast food is the value option that many American families are used to eating anymore.==

For $14, I might be able to go to my local taqueria or my casual sit-down spot, and I would get a way better meal and definitely healthier, too.

That’s because while the price gap between fast food and casual dining has almost completely closed, the quality gap, I feel like, is still pretty massive.

==I would much rather pay $14 to $15 to a local restaurant knowing that I’m getting real food rather than to a fast food chain that’s probably shipping frozen food in from their factories from across the country.==

Another type of fast food that’s gone up a lot in price, especially in the last 10 years, is pizza.

I have definitely spent $40 for a pizza in the last year. And I just thought that that was a ripoff for what it actually was.

The only saving grace out of all of this is probably Costco. They still have the $1.50 hot dog deal, and their pizzas are usually about $10.

***This story is not sponsored by Costco, but I still love the fact that they have value items, especially at their food court.***

### 10\. Unfortunately, Concerts

*10 years ago, you could have bought a concert ticket to a pretty big artist for between $50 to $100 for the night.*

*Now, that wasn’t exactly cheap, but it was still doable for a lot of people and their budgets.*

*In fact, I vividly remember throughout the years 2015 and 2016 going to multiple concerts, and I just thought that it wasn’t that big of a deal in terms of expense.*

But these days, when someone brings up that they want to go to a concert, the immediate place where my mind goes is, *“Okay, is this going to be a $100 concert or is this going to be more like a $200 or $300 or $400 outing?”*

**After you count for the,**

- *Ticket master fees*
- *The transportation getting there*
- *The food or drinks that you’re going to get at the concert*

It might be quite a lot of money. In fact, I went to a concert around Thanksgiving last year, and I remember it being about $50.

And I said to myself in my head, *“Wow, that’s pretty cheap.”* But then I realized that 10 years ago, that would have just been par for the course and maybe even on the expensive side.

I think there are many factors contributing to the fact that concerts are way more expensive.

The first being COVID, during that time, a lot of concerts weren’t being held, and so when concerts finally started back up again, there was so much pent-up demand.

People had all this money they weren’t spending during COVID, and now they were willing to spend more for a concert ticket.

*Now I also want to blame Taylor Swift. Yes, I’m going to blame her because when she came out with the Aerys tour after cancelling her tour that was supposed to happen during COVID, the prices for these tickets just became astronomical.*

Social media magnified and normalized the price that people were willing to pay to go see her.

And so I think that really anchored a lot of people who were online at the time, and they just think that concerts just cost that much now.

Then, finally, I also blame companies like Ticketmaster. **Service fees, processing fees, facility fees, delivery fees, but guess what?**

**It’s still just a digital ticket. So, what are the fees even for?**

These days, it’s not uncommon to see a $200 concert ticket on Ticketmaster, and then all of a sudden it’s $275 after all the fees are said and done.

The alternative I would recommend here is to see a local artist or a smaller venue show.

So, sometimes paying $20 to see an up-and-coming artist in a 300-person room is going to be a way better experience than seeing a megastar for $400 where you have to sit in the nosebleed section of a stadium.

**So, that’s it. Those are the 10 things I think are no longer worth your money in 2026.**
